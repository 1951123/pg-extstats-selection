\begin{abstract}
Cardinality estimation errors are the main cause of suboptimal execution
plans and performance degradation in PostgreSQL. Multivariate extended
statistics mitigate bias stemming from the column-independence assumption but
demand heavy manual tuning. Existing statistics advisors only select column
combinations (the what dimension), ignoring differentiated sampling-capacity
allocation (the how-much dimension), and cannot balance estimation accuracy
and resource usage under limited storage budgets. Overlapping statistics
further introduce severe planner interference and extra ANALYZE maintenance
costs.
Through extensive workload-driven evaluation, we identify a fundamental
per-query property termed one-stat sufficiency: most single-table queries gain
nearly all possible cardinality improvements from at most one well-chosen
multivariate statistic. For most evaluated queries, additional statistics bring
negligible marginal benefits yet worsen planner interference and maintenance
overhead.
Motivated by this observation, we propose Catalog-Mask, an isolation protocol
that eliminates cross-statistic interference to measure each candidate's
independent storage cost and error-reduction gain. We model statistic tuning as
a budget-constrained integer-programming problem to jointly optimize
column-combination selection and per-statistic sampling-capacity assignment.
Our experiments demonstrate that our method significantly reduces Q-error
under tight storage budgets while avoiding redundant statistics and planner
interference. We further characterize applicability boundaries and analyze the
mismatch between storage-based budgeting and real-world ANALYZE overhead,
offering empirical support and a practical tuning paradigm for PostgreSQL
extended statistics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cardinality estimation lies at the heart of relational query optimization.
Inaccurate cardinality estimates frequently produce sub-optimal execution plans
and severely degrade query performance. A major source of estimation error
originates from the ubiquitous column-independence assumption, which ignores
real-world attribute correlations within tables. PostgreSQL provides
multivariate extended statistics, including multi-column MCV, n-distinct, and
functional-dependency statistics, to mitigate such bias. Unfortunately, these
statistics require manual identification of correlated column-sets and careful
configuration, imposing heavy tuning burdens on database administrators.

Prior physical-design tools and statistic-recommendation systems automate the
selection of promising column combinations from query workloads
\cite{chaudhuri2001automating,ilyas2004cords}. Nevertheless, existing solutions
suffer from two fundamental limitations. First, they only address the ``what''
dimension --- deciding which column-sets should form statistics. Sampling
capacity, which controls the estimation quality and storage footprint of each
individual statistic, is treated as a global fixed parameter and never
optimized per candidate. This ``what-only'' strategy cannot realize fine-grained
resource trade-offs under constrained storage budgets:
\[
  \underbrace{\text{which }(\text{table},\text{columns})}_{\text{what}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{which }\texttt{statistics\_target}}_{\text{how much}}.
\]
Second, co-deployed overlapping multivariate statistics introduce negative
planner interference, which distorts cardinality estimates and degrades overall
effectiveness. Without an isolation measurement mechanism, existing work can
only observe aggregated end-to-end quality and cannot quantify the independent
benefit of each candidate statistic; interference and redundancy remain hidden
from optimization formulations.

\emph{Running example.} On the high-cardinality \texttt{posts} table, the
statistic \texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} can be stored at
any level of the capacity menu $\capmenu$ \cite{pgstats}:
at the default target 100 the list is too small to hold the 3-column
correlation (q-error stays $\sim$4.7), while at 10000 it drives the error to
$\approx$1.0. So ``should we build this statistic?'' admits no answer without
``\emph{at what capacity}?'' --- spending on \emph{adjusting} its capacity
competes directly with spending on \emph{adding} another combination, so
capacity is a genuine two-sided decision that must be made jointly with which
statistics to build.

Motivated by this gap, we conduct extensive workload-driven evaluations and
identify an empirical property we term \emph{one-stat sufficiency}. For most
single-table queries, nearly all achievable cardinality-estimation improvement
can be obtained by deploying at most one well-chosen multivariate statistic;
additional statistics often yield negligible marginal gains while amplifying
planner interference and ANALYZE maintenance overhead (a single well-chosen
statistic
already captures $\ge$0.9 of the best multi-statistic gain for every query with
repair room; \S\ref{sec:rq2}). This finding reshapes our understanding of
extended-statistics tuning: effective tuning favors \emph{compact, precise}
statistic deployment rather than blindly accumulating as many statistics as
possible. Moreover, it has a formal consequence --- restricted to at most one
statistic per query, the objective has no interaction term and is exactly linear
(Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}) --- which makes the optimization tractable.

Measuring the independent benefit of each candidate statistic becomes essential
given the above observation, yet simultaneous deployment causes coupled
interference. We propose \emph{Catalog-Mask}, an isolation protocol that
eliminates cross-statistic interference and reliably quantifies the independent
storage cost and error-reduction gain of each candidate column-set. Using these
cost-benefit measurements, we formulate statistic tuning as a
budget-constrained integer program that jointly optimizes two dimensions ---
column-combination selection (what) and per-statistic sampling-capacity
assignment (how-much) --- maximizing overall estimation quality under a given
storage budget.

We conduct comprehensive experiments across multiple real and synthetic
workloads. The results demonstrate that our approach substantially reduces
Q-error and suppresses redundant, interfering statistics under tight storage
budgets. We explicitly acknowledge an important modeling assumption: our
optimizer uses storage consumption as the budget metric, which exhibits
inherent mismatch against real-world ANALYZE maintenance overhead. We further
characterize applicability boundaries for our technique to avoid over-claiming
its practical scope.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\textbf{(1)} We discover and quantitatively validate the one-stat-sufficiency
property over real-world workloads; our end-to-end evaluation further
highlights the negative planner-interference effect introduced by co-deployed
overlapping multivariate statistics. \textbf{(2)} We design Catalog-Mask, an
isolation protocol that enables reliable measurement of the independent
cost-benefit of each candidate statistic, resolving the coupled-measurement
bottleneck. \textbf{(3)} We present a joint what-and-how-much integer-programming
formulation that simultaneously selects column-sets and assigns differentiated
sampling capacity for PostgreSQL multivariate statistics. \textbf{(4)} We
perform extensive end-to-end evaluation over real and synthetic workloads,
analyze the mismatch between storage-based budgeting and real ANALYZE
maintenance cost, and provide practical tuning insights for PostgreSQL
extended statistics.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:back}

\subsection{Cardinality Estimation and Multi-column Correlation}

Query optimizers plan around estimated cardinalities. PostgreSQL's default
estimator uses per-column histograms and most-common-value (MCV) lists and
assumes columns are independent; when columns are correlated this assumption
over- or under-counts by orders of magnitude. \emph{Extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) address this by capturing multivariate structure,
in three kinds --- MCV lists, \texttt{ndistinct}, and functional
\emph{dependencies}. Empirically the MCV kind is the most effective for
selection selectivity, so in this paper we focus on MCV statistics.

\subsection{How PostgreSQL Actually Uses Extended Statistics}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Several mechanism facts (verified from the PostgreSQL 16 source) ground our
model. \textbf{Scope.} Extended statistics are consulted only for
\emph{restriction} (WHERE) clauses on a \emph{single relation}:
the selectivity entry point is gated by a single-relation check, and
\texttt{eqjoinsel} never consults \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext}. Consequently
\textbf{extended statistics do not directly estimate cross-relation join
selectivity} --- they act on single-table restriction clauses, so their direct
effect is confined to single-relation selection predicates (they can influence a
join plan only indirectly, through base-cardinality estimates). This is why our
evaluation separates single-table workloads (where they act directly) from join
workloads (a negative control).

\textbf{Capacity is per-object and tunable.} Each statistic can be assigned its
own \texttt{statistics\_target} via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}
(stored in \texttt{stxstattarget}). The ANALYZE sampling effort is coupled to the
\emph{largest relevant statistics target} on the relation (including the
extended statistics' own targets) \cite{pgstats}; consequently, raising an
extended-statistics target can increase maintenance cost beyond the persistent
storage cost our objective models ($\texttt{ANALYZE}$ reads a larger sample and
builds richer per-column statistics at higher targets; the measured base cost
of a table ANALYZE grows from a few seconds at target 100 to $\approx$22\,s at
10000 in \S\ref{sec:measure-naive}). This couples capacity
to \emph{maintenance cost}, not only storage. Because of this coupling we
deliberately optimize a \emph{storage} budget --- the persistent bytes of the
selected statistics and the estimation quality they deliver --- and treat ANALYZE
maintenance cost separately (it is a property of the maximum, not the sum, of
targets).

\textbf{Capacity-quality trade-off.} \texttt{statistics\_target} is the knob
that sets an extended statistic's precision: for an MCV statistic it caps how
many most-common value-tuples the list stores, and it scales ANALYZE's
sampling effort (\cite{pgstats}). It is a \emph{cap}, not a requirement: a
column-combination with fewer frequent tuples than the cap is fully captured at
any larger target, so raising the target helps only while the list is
\emph{target-capped} (still filling) rather than \emph{data-capped} (bounded by
the data's own cardinality). We therefore model capacity as a small menu of
levels spanning both directions of this axis,
$$\{10, 25, 50, 100, 1000, 10000\},$$
anchored on the PostgreSQL default (100) and extending \emph{below} it (10, 25,
50 --- coarser but cheaper) and \emph{above} it (1000, 10000 --- finer for
higher-cardinality combinations). We include sub-default levels deliberately: a
DBA may legitimately \emph{lower} capacity to save storage on data-capped
combinations, so the decision axis is two-sided and the menu must be wide
enough to expose it. Capacity sets a \emph{per-query} precision floor --- the
``how much'' dimension --- and is a genuine decision axis, which motivates
allocating it under a shared budget. Whether a given combination is best served
by \emph{raising} or \emph{lowering} its capacity is exactly the
data-capped vs.\ target-capped distinction we quantify in \S\ref{sec:rq1};
the two evaluated workloads sit on opposite ends of this axis
(\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}).

\subsection{Related Work}

Prior work on statistics configuration answers essentially \emph{which} column
groups to build. Chaudhuri and Narasayya \cite{chaudhuri2001automating}
identify and prune a workload's needed statistics to a
small effective set; \emph{CORDS} \cite{ilyas2004cords} and \emph{Haas et al.}
\cite{haas2007detecting} discover correlated column groups and rank them
under a catalog-space budget. Production PostgreSQL advisors --- EDB's
Statistics Advisor \cite{edb_query_advisor} (at most two columns, no capacity
tuning) and \texttt{pg\_qualstats} \cite{pg_qualstats} --- likewise recommend
\emph{which} statistics, not \emph{how much} capacity. The shared gap across
this lineage is the capacity axis: none treats PostgreSQL's per-statistic
\texttt{statistics\_target} as a competing resource dimension against a single
shared storage budget, so none decides \emph{whether to upgrade} an
already-chosen statistic from L100 to L1000 to L10000 (``budget allocation''
rather than ``selection''). Budget-constrained \emph{index and view selection}
\cite{chaudhuri1997efficient,bruno2005automatic,agrawal2000automated} is the closest
methodology (cost-driven candidates, MILP/greedy), but its candidates carry no
capacity axis and the objective is not linear. Learned cardinality estimators
\cite{kipf2018learned} are orthogonal: they \emph{change the estimator}, whereas
we leave PostgreSQL's estimator unchanged and optimize the physical statistics
supplied to it, validating the outcome against the \emph{real} planner (no
synthetic model accuracy involved) \cite{chaudhuri1998overview}. Table
\ref{tab:taxonomy} situates our contribution against this lineage.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Notation and Objective}
\label{sec:notn}

We are given a workload of queries $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. The accuracy of the
planner's estimate for $q_i$ is the \textbf{q-error}
\[
  \operatorname{qerr}(\text{est},\text{act})=\frac{\max(\text{est},\text{act})}
  {\min(\text{est},\text{act})}\ge 1.
\]
Let $e_i^0$ be the baseline q-error of $q_i$ with no extended statistics. A
\emph{physical statistic} is $s=(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{level})$,
with storage cost $c_s$ (bytes) and a per-query effective q-error $e_{is}$
when $q_i$ uses only $s$.

\emph{Why a storage budget.} We deliberately optimize \emph{persistent
storage} $c_s$ rather than \texttt{ANALYZE}/maintenance time. PostgreSQL couples
sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} statistics target on a relation, so
maintenance cost is not a per-statistic additive quantity that any selection
model can budget over; storage, by contrast, is a clean additive resource that
every selected statistic consumes and that competes for a shared budget
(\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). We therefore frame the problem as \emph{storage-budgeted
selection} and treat maintenance-aware optimization as a separate extension
(\S\ref{sec:disc-objective}). The selection objective is
\[
  \min_{\{y_s\},\{x_{is}\}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{qerr}_i\big(T_i\big)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_s c_s\,y_s \le C,
\]
where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ says statistic $s$ is created and $T_i$ is the set of
created statistics used by $q_i$. Because a statistic can serve many queries,
we use the assignment $x_{is}\le y_s$ so each \emph{physical} object is built
once and its storage paid once in the budget --- the shared-resource semantics
that make global selection different from per-query greedy.

\subsection{The General Multi-select Problem Is Intractable to Measure}
\label{sec:multi}

Read naively, the objective over $T_i$ is a \emph{combinatorial} problem: each
query could use any subset of its candidate statistics, and the joint q-error
$e_i(T_i)$ of a \emph{set} $T_i$ depends on how those statistics interact in the
planner. Optimizing over $\{y_s\}$ then requires knowing $e_i(T)$ for arbitrary
subsets --- which in general cannot be composed from the per-statistic values
$e_{is}$. Even \emph{measuring} all subsets is infeasible, let alone optimizing
over them.

A standard workaround is the multiplicative approximation in log space
\cite{ioannidis2003history},
\[
  \log e_i(T_i)\;\approx\;\log e_i^0 + \sum_{s\in T_i}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{e_{is}}{e_i^0}\Big),
\]
which treats each statistic's contribution as independent. This is exact only
when the chosen statistics act independently, and can break when overlapping
statistics coexist (we return to this in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\subsection{One-Stat Sufficiency: An Exactly-Linear Special Case}
\label{sec:sparse}

Our measurements (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) reveal that per query a
\emph{single dominant statistic} already captures nearly all improvement
attainable from the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates, which motivates a
\emph{restricted} model: a budgeted statistic-selection problem in which each
query selects at most one statistic, $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$. This \emph{one-stat
sufficiency} is \emph{empirically justified}, not a modeling assumption that
holds by definition: it is exact conditional on the one-statistic-per-query
regime, and \S\ref{sec:rq2} characterizes when that regime holds and when it
fails. Within it, the restriction removes the interaction that makes the general
problem hard: with at most one statistic per query, $e_i(T_i)$ is either $e_i^0$
(no statistic) or $e_{is}$ (the single selected statistic), so no joint term
arises.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linearity of the singleton-effect surrogate]
\label{prop:linear}
Consider the \emph{surrogate} model in which a query's error is $e_i^0$ if no
statistic is assigned and $e_{is}$ if it is assigned the single statistic $s$
(i.e.\ the per-query error \emph{as the model computes it}, not necessarily what
the real planner produces). If each query is assigned at most one statistic
($\sum_s x_{is} \le 1$), then the surrogate objective is exactly
\[
  e_i\big(T_i\big)\;=\;e_i^0\;-\;\sum_s \big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)\,x_{is},
\]
with no interaction term, so the surrogate workload objective is linear in the
selection variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a fixed query $i$, the assigned set $T_i$ is either empty or a singleton
$\{s\}$. In the former case the surrogate error is $e_i^0$; in the latter,
$e_{is}$. Since at most one $x_{is}$ equals $1$, no two statistics co-occur and
there is no joint term. This is a statement about the \emph{model}; whether the
real planner reproduces $e_{is}$ for the chosen $s$ is a separate, empirical
question answered by the E2E validation (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\end{proof}

The log-space approximation used for multi-select is therefore unnecessary for
the sparse surrogate; we solve the linear form exactly. This is the sense in
which the integer program promised in the introduction (\S\ref{sec:intro})
specializes here: because one-stat sufficiency removes the interaction term, the
general integer program degenerates to an \emph{exactly linear} MILP under the
singleton-effect restriction, and \S\ref{sec:p1} then determines when that
surrogate remains faithful to PostgreSQL's actual statistic selection after
deployment. We call this the \emph{capacity-aware} selection problem: because
each capacity level is a distinct physical statistic, choosing \emph{which}
level of a combination to build is expressed by selecting the corresponding
$y_{(table,cols,level)}$, subject to a global budget.

\section{Optimization: Sparse MILP with Capacity Levels}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsection{Variables and Objective}

The MILP decides \emph{which physical statistics to build}; the variables are
$y_s$ (create physical statistic $s$) and $x_{is}$ (query $i$ uses $s$ in the
surrogate, of \S\ref{sec:sparse}). By Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}, minimizing mean
q-error is equivalent to maximizing total improvement,
\[
  \max \sum_{i,s}\underbrace{\big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)}_{\Delta_{is}\ge 0}x_{is},
\]
where an option with $\Delta_{is}\le 0$ (no better than baseline) is pruned in
advance.

\subsection{Constraints}

The MILP enforces: (1) the storage budget $\sum_s c_s y_s\le C$; (2) a statistic
must exist to be used, $x_{is}\le y_s$ (shared across queries); (3) sparse
per-query cap $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$; and (4) capacity-level exclusivity
$\sum_{\ell} y_{(cols,\ell)}\le 1$ (one level per column-combination). A fifth,
optional constraint enforces \emph{global disjointness}: $y_a+y_b\le 1$ when
the column sets of $a,b$ overlap. We evaluate two deployment variants: the model
\emph{with} (5) --- the \emph{deployment-valid} model, whose pairwise
column-disjoint set lets the planner's per-candidate predictions compose after
deployment (\S\ref{sec:p1}) --- and the model \emph{without} it (overlap
allowed, which optimizes the surrogate more aggressively but may require
post-selection validation; Option B, \S\ref{sec:disc-fix}).

\subsection{Pruning and Solve Scalability}
\label{sec:scale}

Two factors keep the MILP small. First,
\texttt{skip\_}\allowbreak\texttt{worse\_}\allowbreak\texttt{than\_baseline}
drops any option whose per-query q-error is not better than the no-stat
baseline; because baseline estimates are usually already good, most options are
pruned, leaving only genuine ``repairers''. Second, global deduplication means
$n_{\text{stats}}$ is the deduplicated universe of
$(\text{table},\text{cols},\text{level})$ rather than the per-query sum. On the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the resulting problem is large but trivially
solvable: the worst-case (no pruning) instance over the full six-level menu
$\{10,\dots,10000\}$ has 115{,}470 physical
statistics, 185{,}136 options and 300{,}606 binary variables and is solved
optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.0s; with pruning it shrinks to 69{,}431
statistics / 89{,}727 options / 159{,}158 variables ($\approx$2.8s). Thus the
optimization is not the bottleneck at full-workload scale --- the phase-1
\emph{measurement} is (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\section{Measurement Infrastructure}
\label{sec:measure}

Our MILP needs a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error $e_{is}$ and size $c_s$
for every option that survives pruning. This section turns the measurement
from an afterthought into a first-class systems component: it is, in fact, the
pipeline's dominant cost, whereas \S\ref{sec:rq3} quantifies how cheap the
optimization itself is. We take $c_s$ as the on-disk byte
size of the candidate's MCV payload --- the serialized list in the
\texttt{stxmcv} column of its \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data} row, read with
\texttt{pg\_column\_size} --- so a coarser capacity is simply a smaller
serialized list, and a list large enough to be stored out-of-line (TOAST) is
counted by the same call.

\subsection{Naive Measurement Is Infeasible on Wide Tables}
\label{sec:measure-naive}

The obvious protocol (\emph{Protocol-A}) isolates a candidate by
\[
  \text{CREATE} \to \text{ANALYZE} \to \text{EXPLAIN} \to \text{DROP} \to \text{ANALYZE},
\]
a clean, drop-analyzable cycle that costs two ANALYZEs per candidate. On a wide
table this is prohibitive. On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table (69 columns,
$\sim$2.5M rows) a single ANALYZE at \texttt{statistics\_target}\allowbreak$=10000$ takes
$\approx$22s with no statistics (warm cache; a fixed base cost), growing
roughly linearly with the number of statistics already on the table
(\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}). Consequently the naive protocol
costs $\approx$44s per candidate --- two ANALYZEs $\times$ $\approx$22s. Even
after deduplication the full workload leaves 19{,}245 distinct candidates, so
Protocol-A would need $\approx$9.8 days of ANALYZE time at this capacity alone
--- not a tractable phase-1.

Crucially, this infeasibility is \emph{not} an accident of an arbitrarily
large menu: the $\approx$22s per-ANALYZE base cost is the necessary
consequence of fixing the single-column statistics at a generous target so
that the baseline is fixed and reproducible --- a comparability requirement,
not a claim of exact determinism given ANALYZE's sampling
(\S\ref{sec:measure-determinism}). One cannot buy it away by shrinking the
target without sacrificing that reproducibility. Protocol-M is therefore the
mechanism that makes a principled (expensive-but-stable) measurement
tractable, rather than a way to paper over an unnecessarily high cost.

\subsection{The Catalog-Mask Protocol (Protocol-M)}
\label{sec:measure-mask}

Our \textbf{catalog-mask protocol} builds all of a table's candidate statistics
\emph{once}, in a single ANALYZE (sampled at the maximum target), and measures
each candidate in isolation by \emph{masking} the others (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask}).
Masking NULLs the
other candidates' payload in \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data}; the planner's
\texttt{statext\_is\_kind\_built()} test then omits a NULL-payload statistic from
\texttt{rel->statlist}, so the planner behaves as if that statistic did not
exist --- an implementation-exact isolation for PostgreSQL 16 that costs no
ANALYZE. Payloads are restored from a
temporary backup table via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM} (the \texttt{pg\_mcv\_list}
type has no bytea cast, so the round-trip must happen inside the server).
Multiple capacity levels are measured with one object per (candidate, level)
via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}, again built by a single
ANALYZE at the maximum target. Protocol-M therefore replaces two ANALYZEs per
candidate with \emph{one} mask--EXPLAIN--restore cycle per candidate, whose cost
is dominated by the catalog UPDATE and grows with the number of payloads
mounted on the table (a tunable granularity; \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}) ---
not by a fixed EXPLAIN. The saving is about amortizing the fixed ANALYZE
base cost (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}): a table's sample and per-column
statistics are recomputed on every ANALYZE, so Protocol-A pays that base twice
per candidate, whereas Protocol-M pays it once per batch of candidates.

\subsection{Correctness of Masked Measurement}
\label{sec:measure-correct}

Masking must reproduce what a \emph{single} deployed statistic would deliver.
We validate this directly by measuring each candidate's q-error twice: (i)
\emph{singleton}, building only that statistic, and (ii) \emph{masked}, building
the candidate plus three overlapping siblings in one ANALYZE and NULLing the
siblings' payloads. The two agree to the last digit every time --- e.g.\
\texttt{FavCnt+ViewCnt} and \texttt{AnsCnt+ViewCnt} at L10000 both read
1.0012 and 1.0129 singleton vs.\ masked; st.144 masked 1.04 vs.\ singleton
1.042 (\S\ref{sec:e2e}). Together with the fact that a NULL payload is ignored
by the planner, this confirms Protocol-M is an \emph{exact} isolation of each
candidate's effect -- not an approximation.

\textbf{Pragmatics and safety.} Protocol-M writes
\texttt{pg\_}\allowbreak\texttt{statistic\_}\allowbreak\texttt{ext\_data}
directly, so it requires superuser and must
run in a dedicated, disposable database cluster (or with autovacuum and
concurrent writes suspended) to avoid contaminating a production system. Two
system facts make the manipulation safe and correct in practice:
(i) \emph{No stale catalog state} --- every UPDATE triggers standard cache
invalidation, and we re-issue a fresh \texttt{EXPLAIN} per candidate, so we
never reuse an estimate computed from a pre-update cache; and
(ii) \emph{update-in-place, not rewrite} --- NULLing a payload does not re-run
ANALYZE, so masking cost is independent of table \emph{size} (row count); it is,
however, linear in the number of payloads mounted on the table, which is why
measurement granularity matters (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}).

\emph{Protocol-M's advantage grows with table size.} The masking step's
independence of row count is what makes the protocol scalable: the ANALYZE
cost $T(N)=B_0+cN$ of \S\ref{sec:measure-naive} has a base term $B_0$
proportional to table size, which the classical per-candidate protocol pays
$2N$ times but Protocol-M pays once, so the saving is
$(2N-1)\,B_0$ --- a \emph{monotone increasing function of dataset scale}.
Our two benchmarks (CENSUS $\approx$740\,MB; the small \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single}
tables, largest $\approx$9\,MB) are both \emph{small} by production standards;
at multi-hundred-GB scale the per-candidate protocol becomes infeasible and
Catalog-Mask the \emph{only} tractable path. Because it relies on the system
catalog layout and the planner's OID-order selection (\S\ref{sec:switch}),
Protocol-M is specific to PostgreSQL 16 and to \emph{MCV} statistics --- honest
limits of the implementation, not of the model (\S\ref{sec:disc-objective}).

\subsection{Measurement Cost and Granularity}
\label{sec:measure-runtime}

\emph{Deterministic single-column baseline.}\label{sec:measure-determinism}
Because \texttt{ANALYZE} is sampling-based, we fix the per-column
\texttt{statistics\_target} at 10000 rather than PostgreSQL's default of 100,
for three reasons: \emph{determinism} (target 100 leaves a $1$--$2\%$ run-to-run
drift that would make \emph{relative} q-error comparisons unreliable, whereas
target $\ge$1000 is exactly reproducible, CV $0$); \emph{completeness} (at low
targets high-cardinality statistics can be built \emph{empty}, a \texttt{NULL}
payload --- e.g.\ the single-MCV estimate of st.144 rises from 4{,}405 at
target\,1000 to the correct $\approx$12{,}391 at target\,10000 --- risking a
degraded baseline); and \emph{attribution} (with a maximally precise
single-column baseline, any improvement from an extended statistic is a
\emph{pure} effect of the correlation it captures, not of patching a coarse
per-column estimate). All per-candidate q-errors $e_{is}$ are measured against
this \emph{same} baseline, so capacity is the only axis that changes between
levels. This makes the single-column ANALYZE \emph{expensive} on a wide table (a
CENSUS ANALYZE at target\,10000 costs $\approx$22\,s), precisely what forces the
catalog-mask protocol (\S\ref{sec:measure-naive}, \S\ref{sec:measure-mask}) ---
the large fixed target and Protocol-M are two halves of one design decision;
consequently most queries have no candidate, their predicates served by the
fixed single-column statistics with baseline q-error already near 1.

\emph{Protocol-M is a large-sample oracle, not a deployment emulator.}
Fixing the single-column baseline at a generous target means every capacity
level $L$ is evaluated on the same \emph{large} sample ($\sim$3M rows), even
though a real ``\texttt{SET STATISTICS} $L$'' deployment builds that candidate
on the smaller sample its own target implies. Protocol-M thus measures a
\emph{conditional large-sample oracle} view $e_{is}(L)$, which isolates capacity
as the only axis (\S\ref{sec:measure-determinism}) but is not a claim that real
deployment reproduces it exactly; we assess deployment fidelity in
\S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}.

\emph{Batch granularity.}
Protocol-M dominates Protocol-A by replacing per-candidate ANALYZEs with
per-candidate masking, but \emph{masking is not free}: it rewrites the system
catalog. Per (candidate, level) the measurement loop is
\[
  \underbrace{\text{mask all but one payload}}_{\text{UPDATE }\times\,N}\;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{EXPLAIN}}_{\approx 0.002\text{s}}\;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{restore all payloads}}_{\text{UPDATE }\times\,N}.
\]
We measured each component on CENSUS \texttt{climate} (Tab.~\ref{tab:measurement}):
the mask and restore are \emph{linear in the table's payload count}
($\approx0.0008$\,s / $\approx0.00003$\,s per payload), while the EXPLAIN is a
constant $\approx0.002$\,s. So one masked measurement costs
$\epsilon(N)\approx0.0008\,N$\,s --- masking is \emph{not} constant: it grows
with the number of statistics mounted during that measurement, a property of
the measurement \emph{granularity}, not of Protocol-M per se.

Group the queries' candidates into $B$ batches; batch $b$ mounts $N_b$ distinct
(candidate$\,{\times}\,$level) objects, ANALYZEs once, and measures $M_b$ cells
by masking against the $N_b$ payloads. The total time is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-cost}
  T(B) \;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(B_0 + c\,N_b\big)}_{\text{one ANALYZE per batch}}
      \;+\;\underbrace{\mu\sum_{b=1}^{B} M_b\,N_b}_{\text{mask + restore}}
      \;+\;\underbrace{\epsilon\,M_{\text{tot}}}_{\text{EXPLAIN}},
\end{equation}
where $B_0$ is the table's warm-cache \texttt{ANALYZE} base (per dataset: on
CENSUS $B{=}22$\,s \emph{empty} and $\approx$113\,s with a query's statistics
mounted; on the small \texttt{posts} table $\approx$1.9\,s), $c$ the per-statistic
\texttt{ANALYZE} increment, and $\mu{\approx}0.0008$\,s per payload. The two
extremes are per-query ($B{=}n$) and workload-wide ($B{=}1$).

\emph{Measurement granularity.}\label{prop:batch}
Because mounting more payloads makes every masked measurement scan a longer
catalog, grouping into large batches amortizes the fixed ANALYZE base but
increases the masking term. Under a uniform model ($N_b{=}\rho M$) $T(B)$ is
strictly convex and minimized at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-opt}
  B^{*} \;=\; M_{\text{tot}}\,\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu\rho}{B_0}},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  m^{*} \;=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{B_0}{\mu\rho}}\ \text{meas.\ per batch},
\end{equation}
so the optimal granularity \emph{grows} with the ANALYZE base and \emph{shrinks}
as masking or sharing rises; a workload-wide build is optimal only under very
high sharing, so on CENSUS we sub-batch instead, splitting candidate-dense
queries into $\approx$55-candidate sub-batches (Tab.~\ref{tab:measurement}). The
same $m^{*}=\sqrt{B_0/\mu}$ optimum applies \emph{within} a query; on the most
candidate-dense CENSUS query (\texttt{query.335}, $n{=}455$) sub-batching into
$7$ pieces reduces measurement from $181.4$\,s to $63.4$\,s ($2.9\times$).
Against Protocol-A's per-candidate cost, the measured speedup is
$\approx$283$\times$ on CENSUS and $\approx$36$\times$ on
\texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} (lower bounds, since Protocol-A's per-candidate
\texttt{ANALYZE} is no more than the table's base). Thus Catalog-Mask --- not
the MILP --- is the enabling component that makes the candidate $\times$
capacity space measurable, and the granularity analysis turns it from a fixed
trick into a tunable component whose operating point is chosen by a measurable
cost model.

\section{Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\textbf{Setup.} We evaluate on PostgreSQL 16.14. \emph{Primary:} \texttt{CENSUS}
(a 69-column table, 468 queries) and \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} (632
single-table sub-plans over StackExchange) --- where extended statistics act
directly; \texttt{stats\_CEB} (146 joins) is the single-table negative control
and \texttt{job-light} join sub-plans \cite{kipf2018learned} a join-oriented one
(Table \ref{tab:bench}). \emph{Synthetic} tables stress specific mechanisms
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}, \S\ref{sec:e2e}). Candidates are the 2-/3-column subsets of
each query's selection predicates --- they directly exercise single-relation
correlation while keeping the candidate space tractable (e.g.\ 19{,}245
distinct combinations on CENSUS); higher-order candidates are an explicit
limitation (\S\ref{sec:disc}). We evaluate the full capacity menu
$\capmenu$ with the per-column baseline fixed at
\texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 (\S\ref{sec:measure}), so capacity is a
free, two-sided decision axis; where a single operating point is needed we
report 1000. We organize the findings around the three claims of the
introduction --- capacity matters, one statistic usually suffices, and joint
optimization helps --- followed by a scope control.

The \emph{mean} q-error on stats\_CEB\_single is only $\approx$1.1 --- but this
is not a marginal problem. q-error is \emph{multiplicative}, so its cost
concentrates in the worst estimates: a query wrong by a factor of 10 or 1000
(not 10\%) is what makes the optimizer pick a categorically wrong plan. The two
primary workloads span this spectrum (Table \ref{tab:bench}): on
\texttt{CENSUS}, 22\% of queries have baseline q-error $>2$ (worst $4{,}172$);
on \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single}, 5.1\% exceed q-error 2 (tail 4.7). The mean
improvement ($1.095\to1.016$) looks small only \emph{because} most queries are
already near q-error 1; the method's value is that it targets and clears that
tail. Hence we report median/P90/max alongside the mean.

\subsection{Capacity Is a Real Decision Axis}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Yes.} Capacity is not a storage knob that can be swept under the rug ---
it sets estimation precision directly. On \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} (the
\texttt{posts} table), a single 3-column MCV,
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)}, repairs a query with
baseline q-error 4.58 only as far as its capacity allows: at target 100 it
does \emph{nothing} (q-error stays 4.58 --- the correlation is not representable
in so few MCV entries), at target 1000 it more than halves the error (2.82), and
at 10000 it becomes near-exact (1.04). Capacity is not always the binding
constraint, however: on the wide CENSUS \texttt{climate} table, most
\emph{data-capped} combinations are already satisfied below the default target
100 --- their MCV lists fill at low cardinality, so raising capacity stores
nothing new, and over the coarse menu $\{100,1000,10000\}$ the capacity axis is
\emph{invisible}. (The workload's worst query, \texttt{query.184} at 4{,}162,
is the rare-case counter-example: it needs L10000 in a real deployment though the
oracle makes it look already-fixed at L100 --- \S\ref{sec:disc-deployment}.)

This contrast is \emph{systematic} once the capacity menu is wide enough to
expose it. Over the coarse menu $\{100,1000,10000\}$ the capacity axis on CENSUS
\emph{appears} absent; but that is an artifact of the coarse menu's lower bound,
because CENSUS's saturation point is \emph{below} the default target 100. When
we extend the menu downward to $\{10,25,50\}$, the axis re-emerges: across the
full 468-query workload, $\approx$29\% of candidates now vary with target and
$\approx$7\% improve by ${\ge}20\%$ as capacity rises from 10 to 50, because
CENSUS's categorical columns are \emph{data-capped} --- their MCV lists fill at
a very low cardinality, so a smaller target genuinely loses entries while a
larger one stores nothing new. Capacity is therefore a genuine two-sided
allocation axis --- a DBA may need to \emph{raise} it on high-cardinality
combinations (the \texttt{posts} case) or find it already satisfied below the
default (data-capped CENSUS) --- so \emph{how much} must be decided jointly with
\emph{which} statistics. (ANALYZE cost likewise grows
with target and installed statistics, \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}.)

\subsection{One Statistic Usually Captures the Attainable Repair}
\label{sec:rq2}

We measure whether one-stat sufficiency survives progressively stronger
attempts to falsify it.

\textbf{Main evidence.} (1) \emph{Real-workload top-$k$.} For each query we
greedily rank non-overlapping candidates by single q-error and measure the
\emph{joint} q-error of keeping the top-1/2/3. On stats\_CEB\_single, adding a
2nd/3rd non-overlapping candidate changes nothing for $93\%$ of the 180 queries
with candidates (the joint $k{=}2,3$ q-error equals $k{=}1$ exactly), and the
largest $k{=}2$-$k{=}1$ gap among the repairable queries (baseline $>1.2$) is
$0.027$; on the wide CENSUS table the two hardest queries are identical at all
$k$ (q.184 $=1.08$, q.382 $=1.12$) --- not an artefact of a small space, since
this holds on the widest, highest-error, most-candidate-dense workload.

(2) \emph{Coverage distribution.} To quantify how much of a multi-statistic gain
a single statistic captures, define the top-1 coverage ratio against the
\emph{measured} joint q-error of the best $k$ non-overlapping subsets (obtained
by the mask protocol, not by a model):
\[
  \operatorname{coverage}_k(i)\;=\;
  \frac{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1\})}{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1,\dots,s_k\})},
\]
where the denominator is the best $k$-subset value (not the singleton), so a
value of $1$ means the single best statistic already realizes the entire
multi-statistic gain --- a non-circular statement. Across the 35 repairable
queries (baseline $>1.2$) this ratio is \textbf{median 1.000} against the best
2-subset, with $100\%$ of such queries exceeding $0.9$; against the best
3-subset it is also median 1.000. By baseline regime, a single candidate reaches
the estimation floor ($\le1.05$) for $97\%$ of the genuinely repairable queries
(base 2--10, $n{=}32$).

(3) \emph{Exhaustive check.} Restricting to one statistic per query might still
lose against the true multi-select optimum, so for every query with few
candidates we enumerate \emph{all} non-empty subsets (173 queries, up to 10
candidates, 18{,}177 subsets) and measure each subset's joint q-error. The best
arbitrary subset never beats the best single statistic by more than $0.0011$ of
absolute joint q-error ($170/173$ exactly equal; the rest differ only in the
third decimal), including the hard ten-candidate queries \texttt{st.562}
(baseline 4.70), \texttt{st.567} and \texttt{st.588}: the exhaustive optimum
equals the singleton --- measured, not assumed.

\textbf{Boundary.} One-stat sufficiency is not universal. (4) \emph{Width
sensitivity.} Relaxing to 4/5-column MCVs on the hardest CENSUS queries, the
best solution is still a \emph{single} statistic (query.62 $1.406\to1.023$),
so the structure survives width relaxation; but the higher-arity candidates
capture more correlation on the hard tail, so our candidate family (and the
reported achievable quality) is an underestimate there --- extending candidate
generation to higher arity is the natural next step, while the sparse MILP is
agnostic to how columns are grouped into candidates. On \texttt{posts} the
2/3-column candidates already reach $\approx$1.0 everywhere, so this effect is
confined to the CENSUS tail.

(5) \emph{Synthetic many-cluster stress test.} We build the controlled failure
test matching the mechanistic hypothesis (\S\ref{sec:disc-sparsity}): on a
synthetic table vary the number $K$ of \emph{mutually independent},
perfectly-correlated column clusters a query filters on, and measure the
top-1 vs.\ top-$K$ coverage ratio
\[
  \operatorname{cov}_K = \frac{e^0 - e(\{s_1\})}{e^0 - e(\{s_1,\dots,s_K\})}.
\]
In this controlled construction, one-stat sufficiency holds exactly only for
$K{=}1$ (one 3-column MCV captures the whole error and a second changes nothing)
and \emph{decays} with $K$ (Table
\ref{tab:sweep}): $\operatorname{cov}_K$ is $1.000$ at $K{=}1$, drops to $0.90$
at $K{=}2$, and collapses to $\approx 0$ for $K\ge3$ --- the monotone failure
regime where the multi-select model (\S\ref{sec:multi}) is required.

\subsection{Joint Optimization Matters Under a Shared Budget}
\label{sec:rq3}

With a \emph{generous} budget the per-query-best greedy \emph{union} (ignoring
the budget) already equals the MILP solution (same 5 statistics, mean 1.021) ---
on independent single-table queries greedy is near-optimal when unconstrained.
The interesting question is under \emph{tight} budgets, where a greedy that
stays within budget spends it on many cheap statistics and cannot reallocate
\emph{capacity}. We compare the MILP against three progressively stronger
budget-aware greedy baselines, all capacity-aware (they may choose
L100/L1000/L10000): \textbf{G1} per-query best improvement-per-byte; \textbf{G2}
a \emph{marginal} shared-resource greedy that re-picks the statistic with the
highest total improvement-per-byte across still-unserved queries after every
selection; and \textbf{G3} an \emph{add-or-upgrade} greedy that additionally
\emph{raises} an already-deployed combination's capacity level (paying only the
price delta). Table \ref{tab:greedy2} reports the MILP against the \emph{best}
of the three at each budget (G1 is the best in all but the most generous case,
so the simpler greedy is not a strawman).

\texttt{statistics\_target} levels across the full two-sided menu
$\capmenu$: under the tightest budgets the solver spends on

The CENSUS workload is the \emph{data-capped} mirror of the same budget-quality
trade-off (cf.\ Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}; its extra panels are shown in the
supplemental material). Its baseline is far worse
(mean 25.19, max 4{,}162 over 468 queries), and a tiny budget already collapses
it: 50\,KB drives the mean from 25.19 to 1.31, the 100\,KB budget cuts the
maximum to 6.2, and 250\,KB brings it to $\approx$3. Beyond $\approx$500\,KB
the curve \emph{saturates}: the mean plateaus at 1.057 and stays there through
2\,MB even as the solver keeps adding more statistics (455 to 457 stats),
because CENSUS's
low-cardinality combinations are already fully captured --- the remaining error
is not repairable by any candidate in the family ($\S\ref{sec:rq1}$). The level
mix is also telling: unlike stats\_CEB\_single, where the solver shifts to
\emph{high} capacity as the budget grows, on CENSUS it spends overwhelmingly on
\emph{low} capacity (L10/L25) even at large budgets --- raising capacity is
rarely worth it once the data-capped lists are already full
($\S\ref{sec:rq1}$). The two curves together span the full ``how much'' axis:
on target-capped, high-cardinality \texttt{posts} statistics, capacity upgrades
are the decisive lever; on data-capped CENSUS statistics, coverage is what
matters and capacity is already satisfied at or below the default.

\textbf{What-only vs.\ what+how-much.} The claim that capacity is a real
decision axis requires isolating it: does
free choice of \texttt{statistics\_target} beat \emph{fixed-capacity
column-only} selection under the \emph{same} budget? We solve the sparse MILP,
at each budget, for four variants on the full 632-query workload: the full
\emph{what+how-much} model (free level choice) and three \emph{what-only}
models that lock every selected statistic to a single level (all L100, all

the full model's mean/max q-error against the \emph{best} what-only variant.

coverage --- the same capacity-reallocation behaviour that separates the MILP
from greedy above. The ``how much'' axis is not a modelling luxury but the
component that buys the last, hardest repairs.

\emph{Optimization overhead.} The optimization is not the bottleneck: on the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the worst-case MILP over the six-level menu
(115{,}470 statistics, 300{,}606 binary variables) solves optimally in
$\approx$4.0\,s and with pruning in $\approx$2.8\,s
(\S\ref{sec:scale}). Measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) dominates the
pipeline, not the solve. \label{sec:rq4}

\emph{Component ablation.} Switching off one component at a time at a fixed
budget isolates each design choice \label{sec:ablation}: disabling the
\emph{capacity} axis (allowing only L10000) hurts at tight budgets (mean q-error
1.027 $\to$ 1.057 at 5\,KB, and 1.0156 $\to$ 1.0233 at 100\,KB), confirming
that capacity allocation --- the ``how much'' dimension --- is load-bearing
(the same advantage of capacity awareness over every fixed what-only level
above, \S\ref{sec:rq3}). In contrast, the sparse per-query cap and the
worse-than-baseline pruning are \emph{free}: they reproduce full quality while
shrinking the problem (pruning cuts 4{,}170 to 2{,}116 variables). So the
ablation separates \emph{modeling assumptions} (capacity; global disjointness
at deployment) from \emph{computational conveniences} (sparse cap; pruning).

\subsection{Scope: Single-Relation Correlation}
\label{sec:rq6}

\textbf{No --- a measured negative control.} On the join-heavy \texttt{stats\_CEB}
workload (146 join queries), deploying each query's best single-table candidate
improves whole-query q-error by a median of only $1.022\times$; the residual
error lives in cross-table join selectivity, which a single-relation MCV cannot
repair (\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). This is the expected null result: the method
does not optimize for coverage's sake.

\section{End-to-End Validation}
\label{sec:e2e}

We close the loop of the measurement $\to$ optimization $\to$ deployment
pipeline by building the MILP-selected sets on the real database. Our
measurement model (\S\ref{sec:measure-correct}) makes two predictions:
(a) an isolated measurement should be exactly predictive, and (b) co-installed
overlapping statistics should not affect each other. We test both causally in
three stages: \emph{Is a single deployed statistic predictable? Is a
co-installed overlapping set predictable? And can we restore predictability?}
Each stage controls the previous one, so together they isolate exactly where
the model is sound and where it is not.

\subsection{Isolated Measurements Are Predictive}
\label{sec:e2e-singleton}

We first confirm that the mask-measured q-error of a single statistic matches
what it delivers when deployed alone. For the statistic
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} the catalog-mask measurement
predicts q-errors of 1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 for queries st.144 / st.588 / st.284.
Building \emph{only} that statistic and re-measuring on the real table gives
1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 --- matching the prediction exactly. This is the
grounding for \S\ref{sec:measure-correct}: per-candidate measurement is sound,
and it validates the economic premise of the whole pipeline (measure each
candidate in isolation, then compose).

\subsection{Overlap Breaks the Independence Assumption}
\label{sec:e2e-coinstalled}

Next we deploy the 2 MB MILP solution for stats\_CEB\_single: five statistics,
all pairwise-overlapping MCVs over \{AnswerCount, FavoriteCount, ViewCount,
PostTypeId\}, predicted mean q-error 1.021. Measured on the real table the mean
is \textbf{1.32} --- a 1.29$\times$ gap. Five of the eight queries match their
prediction (st.562: 1.003, st.308: 1.000, st.182: 1.000, st.398: 1.060, st.314:
1.008), but the three queries served by the \emph{same} statistic
\texttt{(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} degrade from their Stage-1
predictions (1.042/1.029/1.025) to 2.13 / 2.07 / 1.90.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2 for the very same statistic: alone it delivers
1.042; co-installed with four overlapping siblings it gives 2.13.
The \emph{statistic did not change, and its measurement was exact} (Stage 1);
what changed is that the planner now uses \emph{a different} overlapping
statistic --- a second-order interaction among co-installed statistics, exactly
the independence assumption the per-candidate sparse model relies on, and a
joint effect the per-candidate measurement cannot see by construction.

\emph{Why overlap breaks predictability: creation order.} \label{sec:switch}
To verify this optimizer-internal claim directly, we instrument \texttt{EXPLAIN}
on seven overlap-prone queries: for each we take a pair of overlapping
statistics (A = the good one, B = a bad sibling sharing columns) and read the
planner's estimate under three mask conditions --- B-only, A-only, and both live
(A created \emph{before} B, as in a normal deployment). In every case the
co-installed estimate is \emph{bit-identical} to A-only, never a blend and
never B. Crucially, this is \emph{not} because the optimizer ``picks the better
statistic'': the planner traverses its per-relation statistic list in OID
order, which in our deployments matches creation order, and uses the
\emph{first} matching statistic, so building the same pair with B first flips
the winner purely by swapping creation order. Thus, in our instrumented cases,
the co-installed estimate depends on which statistic the planner happens to
reach first rather than on which is intrinsically better; this explains why
overlap can invalidate isolated measurements even when each isolated
measurement is correct. Disjoint deployments (Stage 3) restore predictability
because with no overlapping pairs the ``first-built'' choice is forced.

\subsection{Disjointness Restores Predictability}
\label{sec:p1}

We fix the gap with the Option-A model: a \emph{global-disjointness} constraint
($y_a+y_b\le 1$ whenever the column sets of $a,b$ overlap) forces the deployed
set to be pairwise column-disjoint, so the planner cannot cross-talk. Table
\ref{tab:p1} measures predicted vs E2E for both models at two budgets.

largest budget ($1.018$ at 250\,KB) and largest under small budgets where
overlap is densest ($1.07$--$1.08$ at 10/40\,KB). The residual is a \emph{tail}
rather than a uniform shift: the median overlapping ratio is $1.000$, only
$20/230$ overlapping points exceed $1.05$ --- but those can be severe (worst
$2.05$). This is exactly what a per-candidate measurement model predicts:
individual statistics are faithful, and the residual error is a joint effect of
co-installed overlapping statistics that disjointness removes --- and removes
\emph{generally}, not just in the original two deployments.

\subsection{The Causal Takeaway}
\label{sec:e2e-takeaway}

The three stages form a clean causal chain: per-candidate measurement is sound
and a single deployed statistic is exactly predictable (Stage~1); the only
place prediction diverges is under co-installed \emph{overlapping} statistics,
where the planner's joint choice violates the per-candidate independence
assumption (Stage~2); and enforcing pairwise column disjointness removes that
interaction and restores ratio 1.000 (Stage~3), at a quantified quality cost.
This does not undermine the method --- it precisely delimits where the sparse
model holds and provides a validated, deployable fix. The practical alternative
when the quality cost of disjointness is unacceptable is Option~B
(\S\ref{sec:disc-fix}): solve without the constraint and repair by
EXPLAIN-checking and dropping flagged interferers --- this preserves nominal
quality but does not guarantee predictability (Table~\ref{tab:optionB}).

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{A Mechanistic Interpretation}
\label{sec:disc-sparsity}

A single-table selection query's error is typically dominated by \emph{one}
correlated column cluster: its restriction is a conjunction of predicates whose
selectivity error is largest where independence fails hardest, and a single MCV
over that dominant cluster removes the largest error source while residual
errors in weakly-correlated predicates contribute little. Hence \emph{one}
well-chosen statistic captures nearly all attainable improvement --- consistent
with $k{=}2,3$ adding nothing (\S\ref{sec:rq2}), capacity upgrades mattering
more than additions (\S\ref{sec:rq3}), and the sparse cap costing no quality in
the ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}). It also predicts the boundary: workloads
genuinely driven by \emph{several} independent clusters violate one-stat sufficiency and
warrant the multi-select model (\S\ref{sec:formulation}), confirmed by our
stress test (Table \ref{tab:sweep}); characterising how often that regime
occurs is an open question below.

\subsection{What Is Being Optimized}
\label{sec:disc-objective}

We optimize a \emph{storage} budget (persistent bytes and estimation quality,
\S\ref{sec:rq3}); ANALYZE maintenance cost is treated separately because
PostgreSQL couples sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} target on a relation
(\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}); a maintenance-aware variant is an open question
below. The budget-quality curves (\S\ref{sec:rq3},
Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}) show the budget is, in practice, spent on the
``how much'' axis. A DBA often values the \emph{maintenance} burden over
persistent size, and here one-stat sufficiency helps directly: because each
query needs at most one dominant statistic (\S\ref{sec:rq2}), the selected set
is \emph{small}, so both the number of objects to create and the ANALYZE volume
to refresh them stay low --- maintenance cost is reduced by construction.

\emph{The resulting storage/ANALYZE mismatch.} Because storage is additive
while ANALYZE cost is a property of each relation's \emph{maximum}
\texttt{statistics\_target}, the two can diverge sharply. A \emph{data-capped}
statistic (\S\ref{sec:rq1}) at L10000 stores the same bytes and yields the same
q-error as its L1000 twin, yet it forces PostgreSQL to sample ANALYZE at target
10000 (a $10\times$ larger sample). Quantitatively, at a 100\,KB budget on
stats\_CEB\_single, the storage-only MILP solution and one capped at L1000 have
identical storage (99{,}996 B) and identical mean q-error (1.0156), but
reconstructing them by ANALYZE differs by $\approx$2.7$\times$ (31.4\,s vs.\
11.6\,s); the CENSUS data-capped case is starker (32.0\,s vs.\ 12.2\,s at
10\,KB, with identical storage 9{,}991 B and mean 2.238). The storage-only
objective is thus a genuine but imperfect proxy for maintenance cost; a
maintenance-aware objective (open question~1) is the natural extension.

\emph{Why arithmetic mean q-error.} We use arithmetic mean q-error because it
emphasizes the heavy error tail while preserving the linear MILP objective. A
log-objective produces nearly identical mean q-error across budgets, indicating
that the main conclusions are not sensitive to this choice.

\subsection{Deployment Fidelity of the Oracle}
\label{sec:disc-deployment}

Protocol-M evaluates every capacity level on the same large sample (a
\emph{large-sample oracle} view), which a real ``\texttt{SET STATISTICS} $L$''
deployment need not reproduce. Re-measuring with a real target-specific
\texttt{ANALYZE} (single-column target back at 100), we find the oracle highly
faithful in the ordinary regime: the level it deems optimal and a real
deployment's agree within 1\%, and the headline example
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)}
($4.58\to2.82\to1.04$) is reproduced to within 1\%. The oracle can be
optimistic only on a narrow regime --- when a candidate's columns cover a
query's rare-selectivity driver and the combination is too rare to appear in a
real low-capacity sample, so the large sample captures it while a real small
sample misses it (falling back to base-frequency) with a far worse true
q-error. Sweeping capacity as a continuous knob, the ratio of real to oracle
q-error declines smoothly from $\sim$$10^{2}{-}10^{4}$ when the expected sample
count $\lambda(L){=}(r/N)\cdot\textrm{sample}(L)$ is below one to $\approx$1
once $\lambda$ exceeds roughly 5 --- so we treat low expected counts as an
\emph{empirical warning regime}, not a hard threshold: the harmful optimistic
bias arises when a candidate covers the rare driver AND its expected sample
count is small.

\subsection{The Interference Fix}
\label{sec:disc-fix}

The validated fix (Option A, global disjointness) is the trustworthy main model:
it restores empirically near-exact predictability at a quantified quality cost
(\S\ref{sec:p1}). As a practical fallback (Option B), one can solve without the
constraint and then repair the deployed set. Table~\ref{tab:optionB} measures
both modes end-to-end on the real \texttt{posts} table across three budgets,
reporting the achieved arithmetic-mean q-error over the whole workload and the
number of queries whose E2E/predicted ratio exceeds $1.05$ (``tail'').

nominal quality} of the unconstrained solution --- its achieved mean q-error
($1.019$--$1.031$) is close to the overlap baseline and far better than
Option~A's $\approx\!1.04$ --- but it does \emph{not} eliminate the
predictability tail: dropping the flagged statistics and re-solving fixes the
originally-scary queries yet introduces new interfering pairs as the solver
re-opens the budget, so the residual tail never reaches zero
(Table~\ref{tab:optionB}). Option~A is the only mode with \emph{zero} tail at
every budget, at the price of the quality gap. We recommend A as the default
because it makes the deployment semantics match the measurement model
(\S\ref{sec:measure-correct}) --- the deployment is then structurally
independent, not just measured independently --- and reserve Option~B for
deployments where quality outranks the reproducibility guarantee, expecting to
repeat the solve $\to$ EXPLAIN-check $\to$ drop $\to$ re-solve loop since the
interference is emergent rather than a fixed set of bad statistics.

\subsection{Open Questions}

(1) \emph{Maintenance-aware selection}: jointly modeling ANALYZE cost (a function
of the \emph{max} target) would replace the storage-only budget with an
end-to-end maintenance budget. (2) \emph{When does one-stat sufficiency fail?:}
identifying workloads with genuinely multiple independent correlated clusters
(the regime needing the multi-select model, \S\ref{sec:formulation}) is the
most important open question. (3) \emph{Higher-order candidates}: our
2-/3-column candidate family understates achievable quality on harder CENSUS
queries, where 4-/5-column MCVs help --- extending candidate generation to
higher arity is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Extended-statistics selection should be treated as a joint \emph{which} and
\emph{how much} decision. On the two single-table workloads we evaluate
(stats\_CEB\_single and the wide CENSUS benchmark) we observe one-stat
sufficiency among the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates --- at most one
dominant statistic per query --- which, together with capacity levels, makes the
budget-constrained problem an exactly-linear MILP solvable in seconds at
full-workload scale, via
a catalog-mask protocol that makes per-candidate, per-capacity measurement
feasible on wide tables. End-to-end validation shows per-candidate prediction is
sound and isolates the residual risk to planner cross-talk among co-installed
overlapping statistics; we explain it as a creation-order effect
(\S\ref{sec:switch}) and restore empirically near-exact predictability either
with a conservative global-disjointness mode or with post-selection
planner-validation. Join-heavy workloads are the negative control.

\emph{Scope.} We state precisely what we do and do not claim. Our contribution
is not an MILP \emph{per se}, nor a claim that extended-statistics selection is
inherently sparse. It is the combination of three scoped findings: (i) a
\emph{capacity-aware} reformulation --- the ``what $\times$ how much'' budget
allocation across \texttt{statistics\_target} --- which is new and independent
of sufficiency; (ii) a \emph{measurable one-stat sufficiency}, characterized
empirically on the two evaluated single-table workloads (with an explicit,
synthetic demonstration of where it fails); and (iii) a \emph{scalable
measurement} protocol that turns the candidate $\times$ capacity space into data
the optimizer can consume. Taken together --- not any single one --- these make
capacity-aware extended-statistics selection \emph{optimizable and
deployable} on the workloads we evaluate; the general (Boolean-multi-select,
arbitrary-statistic-kind, join-dominated) version of the problem is explicitly
out of scope.
