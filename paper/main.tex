\begin{abstract}
We observe that PostgreSQL \texttt{CREATE STATISTICS} selection is not just
``which column combinations'' but a joint decision of \emph{which}
(table, columns) and \emph{how much} capacity (each statistic's
\texttt{statistics\_target}). We re-frame it as \emph{capacity-aware
selection}: under a storage budget, should we spend on adding a statistic or
on upgrading an existing one to a higher-capacity target? Across the single-table
workloads we evaluate (stats\_CEB and the wide 69-column CENSUS benchmark) we find a
strikingly sparse structure: one well-chosen multivariate statistic captures
nearly all improvement attainable from the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates
per query. We exploit this by restricting each
query to a single statistic, under which the surrogate objective is
\emph{exactly linear} and the resulting budgeted MILP solves in seconds at
full-workload scale. A catalog-mask protocol makes the enormous candidate $\times$ capacity
space measurable on wide tables. End-to-end validation on PostgreSQL 16 shows
individual candidate measurements are predictive, but reveals that co-installed
overlapping statistics cause planner interference --- motivating global
disjointness or post-selection validation. Join-heavy workloads act as a
negative control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern query optimizers rely on cardinality estimates to choose plans, and when
columns are correlated the default independence assumption can misestimate by
orders of magnitude. PostgreSQL exposes \emph{extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE}\allowbreak\texttt{ STATISTICS}) to capture multivariate
correlation, yet deciding
\emph{which} statistics to create --- and, crucially, \emph{how precisely} to
store them --- is left to the database administrator. This paper asks whether
that choice can be automated, and what structure it has in practice.

\textbf{(A) The problem: a joint ``what and how much'' allocation.} A statistic
is not atomic. Each has a \emph{capacity}, the number of MCV entries it stores,
controlled by its \texttt{statistics\_target} (100, 1000, or 10000). Capacity
determines both storage and estimation precision: a 3-column correlation that is
invisible at target 100 becomes accurate at 1000 and exact at 10000. Automated
statistics management is a well-studied problem (Chaudhuri and Narasayya
\cite{chaudhuri2000automating}), but it chooses \emph{which} statistics to
create. Extended-statistics selection is richer: a storage budget can be spent
either by \emph{adding} a statistic or by \emph{upgrading an existing one}
from L100 to L1000 to L10000. The right question is therefore a
\emph{resource-allocation} one --- given a budget, which
$(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ objects to instantiate --- not
merely which column combinations:
\[
  \underbrace{\text{which }(\text{table},\text{columns})}_{\text{what}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{which }\texttt{statistics\_target}}_{\text{how much}},
\]
compounded by a combinatorial explosion in candidates --- a single CENSUS query
can have up to 455 candidate column combinations (30{,}856 is the sum of
per-query candidate counts before any deduplication; after globally
deduplicating identical column combinations across the workload only 19{,}245
distinct combinations remain). To our knowledge, prior work on automatic
statistics configuration has focused on discovering or selecting useful column
groups; we are not aware of prior work that explicitly treats PostgreSQL's
per-statistic \texttt{statistics\_target} as a competing resource-allocation
dimension under a shared storage budget (\S\ref{sec:back}).

\emph{Running example.} Consider a query filtering
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,}\allowbreak\texttt{ViewCount)}. A statistic over
this combination can be stored at L100, L1000, or L10000. Measured on our
workload, L100 is too
coarse to capture the correlation (q-error stays $\sim$4.7), while L10000
drives it to $\approx$1.0. How much capacity is enough is therefore itself a
decision: ``should we build this statistic?'' admits no useful answer without
``\emph{at what capacity}?''. And because L10000 costs $\sim$100\,KB while L100
costs $\sim$2\,KB, that choice is \emph{quantitative} under a budget. This is
the ``what and how much'' allocation we optimize: the same combination can be
deployed at different capacities depending on the budget, and spending on
\emph{upgrading} it competes directly with spending on \emph{adding} another
combination.

\textbf{(B) The surprise: the benefit is almost entirely sparse.} At first
glance the problem looks like a combinatorial multi-select: a query could
benefit from several statistics at once. Our measurements show otherwise.
Across both single-table workloads (the wide 69-column CENSUS and
stats\_CEB\_single), one well-chosen multivariate statistic captures nearly all
improvement attainable from the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates for a
query: adding a second or third non-overlapping
candidate gives almost no benefit, and on the hardest CENSUS queries the top-1,
top-2 and top-3 joint q-errors are identical. This was not assumed --- it was
\textbf{discovered} --- and it has a direct consequence: \emph{restricted to at
most one statistic per query}, the objective has no interaction term and is
\emph{exactly linear} (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}). We do not claim this makes the
general multi-select problem tractable in the abstract, nor that it describes
the raw deployment objective; rather, sparsity is an
\emph{empirically characterized regime} of the workloads we evaluate, and the
linear model is exact \emph{conditional on} the one-statistic-per-query
restriction that this regime justifies --- for the \emph{surrogate}, with the
E2E experiments (\S\ref{sec:e2e}) determining when that surrogate remains
faithful to PostgreSQL's real statistic selection.

\textbf{(C) The enabler: measuring the massive candidate space.} Instantiating
this optimization requires a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error for every
surviving option. On wide tables the naive protocol (CREATE--ANALYZE--EXPLAIN
per candidate) is infeasible --- a single CENSUS ANALYZE at target 10000 takes
$\sim$20s. Our \emph{catalog-mask protocol} (\S\ref{sec:measure}) builds all
candidates in one ANALYZE and isolates each by masking the others'
\texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data} payloads, cutting measurement cost by
$\sim$60$\times$ and making the enormous candidate $\times$ capacity space
actually measurable. The resulting MILP is then the tractable part: the full
CENSUS workload --- 57{,}735 physical-statistic variables ($y_s$) and 92{,}568
query--statistic assignment variables ($x_{is}$), i.e.\ 150{,}303 binary
variables in total --- is solved optimally in under five seconds
(\S\ref{sec:rq4}) --- optimization is not the dominant runtime bottleneck.

\textbf{(D) The validation surprise: overlapping statistics interfere.}
End-to-end deployment on real PostgreSQL shows per-candidate predictions are
accurate, but co-installed \emph{overlapping} statistics let the planner switch
queries to a suboptimal statistic (predicted mean 1.02 vs.\ measured 1.32). A
\emph{global-disjointness} constraint restores (empirically) near-exact
predictability
(ratio 1.000) at a quantified quality cost (\S\ref{sec:p1}). This is not a
failure of our measurement --- it is a second-order planner interaction that the
per-candidate model cannot see, and a concrete, validated fix.

In summary, we contribute (i) a reformulation of extended-statistics selection
as budgeted \emph{what-and-how-much allocation} (\S\ref{sec:formulation},
\S\ref{sec:milp}); (ii) an empirical sparse-structure finding with a formal
linearity proposition that makes the problem tractable (\S\ref{sec:rq2}); (iii)
the catalog-mask measurement protocol that makes the candidate space measurable
(\S\ref{sec:measure}); and (iv) an end-to-end validation that isolates --- and
fixes --- the planner-interference effect (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:back}

\subsection{Cardinality Estimation and Multi-column Correlation}

Query optimizers plan around estimated cardinalities. PostgreSQL's default
estimator uses per-column histograms and most-common-value (MCV) lists and
assumes columns are independent; when columns are correlated this assumption
over- or under-counts by orders of magnitude. \emph{Extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) address this by capturing multivariate structure,
in three kinds --- MCV lists, \texttt{ndistinct}, and functional
\emph{dependencies}. Empirically the MCV kind is the most effective for
selection selectivity, so in this paper we focus on MCV statistics.

\subsection{How PostgreSQL Actually Uses Extended Statistics}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Several mechanism facts (verified from the PostgreSQL 16 source) ground our
model. \textbf{Scope.} Extended statistics are consulted only for
\emph{restriction} (WHERE) clauses on a \emph{single relation}:
the selectivity entry point is gated by a single-relation check, and
\texttt{eqjoinsel} never consults \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext}. Consequently
\textbf{extended statistics do not directly estimate cross-relation join
selectivity} --- they act on single-table restriction clauses, so their direct
effect is confined to single-relation selection predicates (they can influence a
join plan only indirectly, through base-cardinality estimates). This is why our
evaluation separates single-table workloads (where they act directly) from join
workloads (a negative control).

\textbf{Capacity is per-object and tunable.} Each statistic can be assigned its
own \texttt{statistics\_target} via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}
(stored in \texttt{stxstattarget}). The ANALYZE sampling effort is coupled to the
\emph{largest relevant statistics target} on the relation (including the
extended statistics' own targets) \cite{pgstats}; consequently, raising an
extended-statistics target can increase maintenance cost beyond the persistent
storage cost our objective models (a full scan at 10000). This couples capacity
to \emph{maintenance cost}, not only storage. Because of this coupling we
deliberately optimize a \emph{storage} budget --- the persistent bytes of the
selected statistics and the estimation quality they deliver --- and treat ANALYZE
maintenance cost separately (it is a property of the maximum, not the sum, of
targets).

\textbf{Capacity-quality trade-off.} Measured on the CENSUS \texttt{climate}
table (69 columns), the repair quality of the best single statistic is roughly
2.1--2.3 at target 100, 1.2--2.1 at 1000, and $\approx$1.0 at 10000. Target 100
loses 3-column correlation entirely; target 1000 captures it at a fraction of
the ANALYZE cost of 10000.

\subsection{Related Work}

We position our work against five bodies of literature; for each we say what it
\emph{optimizes}, \emph{measures}, and why it does not solve the
\emph{what} $\times$ \emph{how much} question.

\textbf{(a) Cardinality estimation.} Selectivity estimation is one of the
oldest problems in query optimization \cite{selinger1979access}: histograms
\cite{piatetsky1984accurate,poosala1996improved,ioannidis2003cardinality},
selectivity from query feedback \cite{chendependencies}, and -- more recently --
\emph{learned} cardinality models \cite{kipf2019learned} all aim to improve the
estimate itself. Our problem is orthogonal: we do not estimate, we decide
\emph{which statistics to deploy} so that PostgreSQL's own estimator is
accurate \cite{chaudhuri1998overview}. In particular, we drive the \emph{real}
planner and measure its actual q-error, so no synthetic model accuracy is
involved.

\textbf{(b) Automatic statistics configuration.} Chaudhuri and Narasayya
\cite{chaudhuri2000automating,chaudhuri2001automating} showed that a workload's
needed statistics can be identified and pruned to a small effective set. This
establishes the \emph{which} dimension as well-studied --- and is exactly why we
do \emph{not} claim novelty for ``automatically selecting statistics.'' Two
subsequent lines are the most direct prior art and frame the boundary of our
claim. \emph{CORDS} \cite{ilyas2004cords} automatically discovers correlations
and soft functional dependencies among columns and recommends column-group
statistics to avoid the independence assumption; \emph{Haas et al.}
\cite{haas2007dependencies} detect dependent attribute pairs from query
feedback and \emph{rank} them so that only the most valuable multivariate
statistics are kept under a catalog-space budget. Both pre-frame our concern
(candidate explosion, catalog budget), but both operate at the level of
\emph{which column groups} and \emph{pairwise} independence --- they do not
jointly choose each statistic's \emph{capacity} and do not optimize a shared
storage budget across the \texttt{statistics\_target} axis. Concretely, prior
work decides \emph{whether to add} a statistic; we additionally decide
\emph{whether to upgrade} an already-chosen one from L100 to L1000 to L10000.
That is the shift from ``selection'' to ``budget allocation.'' We are careful to
note that capacity (\texttt{statistics\_target}) \emph{tuning} appears
incidentally in database-tuning practice and knob-optimization literature; what
is missing there is a \emph{joint} treatment in which capacity and column choice
compete for a \emph{single} storage budget, so that upgrading one statistic
trades off against adding another. That joint resource-allocation view, with a
provably linear objective on an empirically validated sparse regime, is our
point of departure.

\textbf{(c) Physical database design and index selection.} Budget-constrained
structure selection is a classic problem: which indexes and materialized views
to build, typically solved by cost-driven candidate generation plus MILP or
greedy \cite{chaudhuri1997index,indexselection,agrawal2000automated}. Our MILP
follows the same methodology. The novelty is twofold: our candidates carry a
\emph{capacity} axis (an index or materialized view has no analogue of
$\texttt{statistics\_target}$), and our objective is \emph{provably linear}
because we exploit a \emph{discovered sparse structure} ($\S\ref{sec:rq2}$) --- a
property index-selection workloads do not exhibit. The result is that the hard
part of the problem is not the optimization (seconds at full scale) but the
\emph{measurement} of the candidate space (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\textbf{(d) PostgreSQL extended statistics.} The mechanics of
\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS} --- MCV / ndistinct / dependencies, per-object
\texttt{statistics\_target}, the restriction to single relations, and the
ANALYZE sampling coupling \cite{pgstats,pgmv} --- are the substrate of our
model (\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). To our knowledge no prior work formulates
extended-statistics \emph{deployment} as a budgeted joint selection-and-capacity
problem, nor provides a catalog-mask protocol to measure the candidate space at
scale.

\textbf{(e) Workload-driven database tuning.} Automated physical tuning
\cite{indexselection}, knob/configuration optimization, and workload-aware
index advisors share our high-level framing (workload $\to$ candidates $\to$
optimizer evaluation $\to$ global selection). What distinguishes our accuracy
criterion is that we optimize \emph{prediction quality under a storage budget}
(mean q-error), not wall-clock latency or a cost-benchmark proxy. Learned
cardinality estimators \cite{kipf2019learned} are orthogonal in the same sense:
they \emph{change the estimator}, whereas we leave PostgreSQL's estimator
unchanged and optimize the physical statistics supplied to it.

\textbf{(f) PostgreSQL-ecosystem statistics advisors.} Production tools already
recommend extended statistics on PostgreSQL workloads: EDB's Query Advisor
\emph{Statistics Advisor} \cite{edbstatsadvisor} monitors multi-column filters
and selectivity-error, explores column combinations, and weights candidates by
benefited-query count, but limits to at most two columns and does not tune
capacity; \texttt{pg\_qualstats} \cite{pgqualstats} collects predicate
statistics that can surface correlated columns for manual extended-statistics
creation. Both answer essentially \emph{which} statistics to recommend; neither
optimizes the joint \emph{which $\times$ how much} (capacity) decision against a
single shared storage budget, nor provides an E2E planner-interference fix.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Notation and Objective}
\label{sec:notn}

We are given a workload of queries $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. The accuracy of the
planner's estimate for $q_i$ is the \textbf{q-error}
\[
  \operatorname{qerr}(\text{est},\text{act})=\frac{\max(\text{est},\text{act})}
  {\min(\text{est},\text{act})}\ge 1.
\]
Let $e_i^0$ be the baseline q-error of $q_i$ with no extended statistics. A
\emph{physical statistic} is $s=(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{level})$,
with storage cost $c_s$ (bytes) and a per-query effective q-error $e_{is}$
when $q_i$ uses only $s$. The selection objective is
\[
  \min_{\{y_s\},\{x_{is}\}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{qerr}_i\big(T_i\big)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_s c_s\,y_s \le C,
\]
where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ says statistic $s$ is created and $T_i$ is the set of
created statistics used by $q_i$. Because a statistic can serve many queries,
we use the assignment $x_{is}\le y_s$ so each \emph{physical} object is built
once and its storage paid once in the budget --- the shared-resource semantics
that make global selection different from per-query greedy.

\subsection{The General Multi-select Problem Is Intractable to Measure}
\label{sec:multi}

Read naively, the objective over $T_i$ is a \emph{combinatorial} problem: each
query could use any subset of its candidate statistics, and the joint q-error
$e_i(T_i)$ of a \emph{set} $T_i$ depends on how those statistics interact in the
planner. Optimizing over $\{y_s\}$ then requires knowing $e_i(T)$ for arbitrary
subsets --- which in general cannot be composed from the per-statistic values
$e_{is}$. Even \emph{measuring} all subsets is infeasible, let alone optimizing
over them.

A standard workaround is the multiplicative approximation in log space
\cite{ioannidis2003cardinality},
\[
  \log e_i(T_i)\;\approx\;\log e_i^0 + \sum_{s\in T_i}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{e_{is}}{e_i^0}\Big),
\]
which treats each statistic's contribution as independent. This is exact only
when the chosen statistics act independently, and can break when overlapping
statistics coexist (we return to this in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\subsection{The Sparse Special Case: Exact and Linear}
\label{sec:sparse}

Our measurements (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) reveal --- surprisingly --- that per query a
\emph{single dominant statistic} already captures nearly all improvement
attainable from the evaluated candidate family. This observation motivates a \emph{restricted} optimization model:
the \emph{sparse} special case, in which each query selects at most one
statistic, $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$. We stress that this is an \emph{empirically
justified restriction}, not a modeling assumption that holds by definition: it
is exact \emph{conditional on} the one-statistic-per-query regime
(\S\ref{sec:rq2} characterizes when it holds and when it fails). Within that
regime, the restriction removes the interaction that makes the general problem
hard: with at most one statistic per query, $e_i(T_i)$ is either $e_i^0$ (no
statistic) or $e_{is}$ (the single selected statistic), so no joint term
arises.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linearity of the singleton-effect surrogate]
\label{prop:linear}
Consider the \emph{surrogate} model in which a query's error is $e_i^0$ if no
statistic is assigned and $e_{is}$ if it is assigned the single statistic $s$
(i.e.\ the per-query error \emph{as the model computes it}, not necessarily what
the real planner produces). If each query is assigned at most one statistic
($\sum_s x_{is} \le 1$), then the surrogate objective is exactly
\[
  e_i\big(T_i\big)\;=\;e_i^0\;-\;\sum_s \big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)\,x_{is},
\]
with no interaction term, so the surrogate workload objective is linear in the
selection variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a fixed query $i$, the assigned set $T_i$ is either empty or a singleton
$\{s\}$. In the former case the surrogate error is $e_i^0$; in the latter,
$e_{is}$. Since at most one $x_{is}$ equals $1$, no two statistics co-occur and
there is no joint term. This is a statement about the \emph{model}; whether the
real planner reproduces $e_{is}$ for the chosen $s$ is a separate, empirical
question answered by the E2E validation (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\end{proof}

The log-space approximation used for multi-select is therefore unnecessary for
the sparse surrogate; we solve the linear form exactly. This makes the
\emph{surrogate optimization problem} an exact MILP under the singleton-effect
restriction; \S\ref{sec:p1} then determines when that surrogate remains faithful
to PostgreSQL's actual statistic selection after deployment. We call this the
\emph{capacity-aware} selection problem: because each capacity level is a
distinct physical statistic, choosing \emph{which} level of a combination to
build is expressed by selecting the corresponding $y_{(table,cols,level)}$,
subject to a global budget.

\section{Optimization: Sparse MILP with Capacity Levels}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsection{Variables and Objective}

Before defining the program, we make explicit the \emph{role} of the model's
variables, because a natural question is whether the system can
really dictate which statistic a query uses. It cannot: PostgreSQL's planner,
not the optimizer, decides at plan time which installed extended statistic
applies to a query. We therefore treat the MILP as an \emph{upper-level
deployment optimization} over a \emph{singleton-effect surrogate model}. The
assignment $x_{is}$ is a \emph{model} variable, not a plan directive: it states
that \emph{if} the surrogate is faithful, query $i$'s error under the deployed
set is $e_{is}$ for the level at which $s$ is created ($\S\ref{sec:sparse}$).
The surrogate is exact when each query's best statistic can be isolated ---
which is what the one-statistic-per-query regime guarantees. The end-to-end
validation (\S\ref{sec:e2e}) is precisely the experiment that checks whether the
surrogate assignment remains faithful after the real planner performs its own
selection; \S\ref{sec:p1} shows it does under pairwise disjointness and does
not under overlapping co-installation.

We solve the sparse model as a mixed-integer linear program. Binary variables
are $y_s$ (create physical statistic $s$) and $x_{is}$ (query $i$ uses $s$
in the surrogate model).
By Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}, minimizing mean q-error is equivalent to
maximizing total improvement,
\[
  \max \sum_{i,s}\underbrace{\big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)}_{\Delta_{is}\ge 0}x_{is},
\]
where an option with $\Delta_{is}\le 0$ (no better than baseline) is pruned in
advance.

\subsection{Constraints}

The MILP enforces: (1) the storage budget $\sum_s c_s y_s\le C$; (2) a statistic
must exist to be used, $x_{is}\le y_s$ (shared across queries); (3) sparse
per-query cap $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$; and (4) capacity-level exclusivity
$\sum_{\ell} y_{(cols,\ell)}\le 1$ --- a real object has a single
\texttt{statistics\_target}, so at most one level of a given combination is
created. A fifth constraint enforces \emph{global disjointness}: $y_a+y_b\le 1$
whenever the column sets of $a$ and $b$ overlap. This is stronger than (4)
(which only forbids two levels of the \emph{same} combination). We call the
model with constraint (5) the \emph{deployment-valid} model: it guarantees the
deployed set is pairwise column-disjoint, so the planner cannot cross-talk among
co-installed overlapping MCVs and the surrogate's per-candidate predictions
remain composable after deployment (\S\ref{sec:p1}). The model without (5)
(overlap allowed) optimizes the surrogate more aggressively but may require
post-selection validation (Option B, \S\ref{sec:disc-fix}).

\subsection{Pruning and Solve Scalability}
\label{sec:scale}

Two factors keep the MILP small. First,
\texttt{skip\_}\allowbreak\texttt{worse\_}\allowbreak\texttt{than\_baseline}
drops any option whose per-query q-error is not better than the no-stat
baseline; because baseline estimates are usually already good, most options are
pruned, leaving only genuine ``repairers''. Second, global deduplication means
$n_{\text{stats}}$ is the deduplicated universe of
$(\text{table},\text{cols},\text{level})$ rather than the per-query sum. On the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the resulting problem is large but trivially
solvable: the worst-case (no pruning) instance has 57{,}735 physical
statistics, 92{,}568 options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved
optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280
statistics / 46{,}392 options / 80{,}672 variables ($\approx$4.3s). Thus the
optimization is not the bottleneck at full-workload scale --- the phase-1
\emph{measurement} is (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\section{Measurement Infrastructure}
\label{sec:measure}

Our ILP needs a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error $e_{is}$ and size $c_s$
for every option that survives pruning. This section turns the measurement
from an afterthought into a first-class systems component: it is, in fact, the
pipeline's true bottleneck (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\subsection{Naive Measurement Is Infeasible on Wide Tables}
\label{sec:measure-naive}

The obvious protocol (\emph{Protocol-A}) isolates a candidate by
\[
  \text{CREATE} \to \text{ANALYZE} \to \text{EXPLAIN} \to \text{DROP} \to \text{ANALYZE},
\]
a clean, drop-analyzable cycle that costs two ANALYZEs per candidate. On a wide
table this is prohibitive. On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table (69 columns,
$\sim$2.5M rows) a single ANALYZE at \texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 takes
$\sim$21.9s with no statistics, and grows with the number of statistics already
on the table (Table \ref{tab:measurement}). Consequently the naive protocol
costs $\sim$44s per candidate --- 44 seconds $\times$ 19{,}245 deduplicated
candidates is not a tractable phase-1.

\subsection{The Catalog-Mask Protocol (Protocol-M)}
\label{sec:measure-mask}

Our \textbf{catalog-mask protocol} builds all of a table's candidate statistics
\emph{once}, in a single ANALYZE (sampled at the maximum target), and measures
each candidate in isolation by \emph{masking} the others (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask}).
Masking NULLs the
other candidates' payload in \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data}; the planner's
\texttt{statext\_is\_kind\_built()} test then omits a NULL-payload statistic from
\texttt{rel->statlist}, so the planner behaves as if that statistic did not
exist --- an implementation-exact isolation for PostgreSQL 16 that costs no
ANALYZE. Payloads are restored from a
temporary backup table via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM} (the \texttt{pg\_mcv\_list}
type has no bytea cast, so the round-trip must happen inside the server).
Multiple capacity levels are measured with one object per (candidate, level)
via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}, again built by a single
ANALYZE at the maximum target. Protocol-M therefore costs one ANALYZE (for the
whole table) plus a cheap EXPLAIN per candidate, instead of two ANALYZEs per
candidate.

\subsection{Correctness of Masked Measurement}
\label{sec:measure-correct}

Masking must reproduce what a \emph{single} deployed statistic would deliver.
We validate this directly: for a statistic that the mask measurement predicts
at q-error $e$, we build \emph{only} that statistic on the real table and
re-measure. The singleton deployment reproduces the masked prediction (e.g.\
st.144: masked 1.04 vs.\ singleton 1.042; st.588 1.03 vs.\ 1.029; st.284 1.02
vs.\ 1.025, \S\ref{sec:e2e}). Together with the fact that a NULL payload is
ignored by the planner, this confirms Protocol-M is not an approximation but an
\emph{exact} isolation of each candidate's effect on the queries we test.

\textbf{Direct mask-vs-singleton check.} To verify that masking exactly
reproduces a singleton deployment --- even with overlapping siblings co-built
in the same ANALYZE --- we measure each candidate's q-error twice: (i)
\emph{singleton}, building only that statistic, and (ii) \emph{masked}, building
the candidate plus three overlapping siblings in one ANALYZE and NULLing the
siblings' payloads. Table \ref{tab:maskcorrect} shows the two agree exactly.

\textbf{Pragmatics and safety.} Protocol-M writes
\texttt{pg\_}\allowbreak\texttt{statistic\_}\allowbreak\texttt{ext\_data}
directly, so it requires superuser and must
run in a dedicated, disposable database cluster (or with autovacuum and
concurrent writes suspended) to avoid contaminating a production system. Two
system facts make the manipulation safe and correct in practice.
(i) \emph{No stale catalog state.} Every UPDATE to the catalog triggers the
usual cache invalidation, so the relcache/syscache entries consulted by the
planner's \texttt{statext\_}\allowbreak\texttt{is\_kind\_built()} test are
rebuilt on the next command; we re-issue a fresh \texttt{EXPLAIN} per
candidate, so we never reuse an estimate computed from a pre-update cache.
(ii) \emph{Update-in-place, not rewrite.} NULLing a payload does not re-run
ANALYZE, so masking cost is independent of the table size and of the number of
candidates. Because the protocol relies on the layout of the system catalog (a
\texttt{pg\_mcv\_list} without a bytea cast, so the round-trip happens inside
the server via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM}), it is specific to PostgreSQL 16; we
have not verified it against other major versions. These are honest limits of
the implementation, not of the model.

\subsection{Runtime: Measurement Is the Bottleneck, Not the Solve}
\label{sec:measure-runtime}

Table \ref{tab:measurement} shows the consequence. Using the measured linear
growth of ANALYZE time with the number of statistics on the table, Protocol-A

ANALYZE plus $N$ cheap EXPLAIN payloads. The gap widens with $N$: a $9\times$
saving at 10 candidates grows to $\sim$60$\times$ at 100 and $\sim$130$\times$
at 1000. For a single Census query the masked measurement completes in minutes
where the naive protocol would take hours. This is why the catalog-mask
protocol --- not the MILP --- is the enabling component that makes the massive
candidate $\times$ capacity space measurable, and why we emphasize that the
optimization (\S\ref{sec:rq4}) is the cheap part of the pipeline.

and \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} (632 single-table sub-plans of the same schema).

near-exact, and report 1000 as the default operating point. We organize
the results around six research questions.

\subsection{RQ1: Does Capacity Materially Affect Repair Quality?}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Yes.} On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table, the best single statistic
brings a high-error query to a q-error of roughly 2.1--2.3 at target 100,

therefore not a storage knob that can be swept under the rug --- it sets the
estimation precision directly. This motivates treating

\subsection{RQ2: Is One Statistic per Query Sufficient?}
\label{sec:rq2}

\textbf{Surprisingly, yes.} For each query we greedily rank non-overlapping
candidates by single q-error and measure the \emph{joint} q-error of keeping the
top-1/2/3. On stats\_CEB\_single, adding a 2nd/3rd non-overlapping candidate
changes nothing for $93\%$ of the 180 queries with candidates (the joint
$k{=}2,3$ q-error equals $k{=}1$ exactly), and the largest $k{=}2$-$k{=}1$ gap
over the whole workload is $0.027$ (Table \ref{tab:multi}); on the wide CENSUS
table the two hardest queries are identical at $k{=}1,2,3$. This is not an
artefact of a small candidate space: it holds on the widest, highest-error,
most-candidate-dense workload in our study.

This is not just anecdotal. Fig.~\ref{fig:sparsity} characterises the 
\emph{distribution} over the whole 632-query workload. The natural sparsity
question is \emph{non-circular}: how much of the improvement of the measured
\emph{best $k$-subset} does a single statistic already capture? We define the
top-1 coverage ratio against the measured joint q-error of the best $k$
non-overlapping subsets (obtained by the mask protocol, not by a model),
\[
  \operatorname{coverage}_k(i)\;=\;
  \frac{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1\})}{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1,\dots,s_k\})},
\]
where $e_i(\{s_1\})$ and $e_i(\{s_1,\dots,s_k\})$ are the \emph{measured} joint
q-errors of the best 1- and $k$-statistic subsets (a value of $1$ means the
single best statistic already realizes the entire multi-statistic gain; the
denominator is not the single-statistic value, so this is not a tautology).
Across the 35 queries with real repair room (baseline $>1.2$), this ratio
against the best \emph{2-subset} has \textbf{median 1.000}, with $100\%$ of such
queries exceeding $0.9$; against the best \emph{3-subset} it is also median
$1.000$. Equivalently, for $93\%$ of the 180 queries with candidates the joint
q-error at $k{=}2$ equals $k{=}1$ exactly, and the largest $k{=}2$-$k{=}1$ gap
over the whole workload is $0.027$. By baseline regime, a single candidate
reaches the estimation floor ($\le1.05$) for $94\%$ of near-baseline queries
(base 1--1.1) and $97\%$ of the genuinely repairable queries (base 2--10, $n{=}32$).
One dominant column cluster drives a single-table query's error, and one
well-chosen 3-column MCV captures it --- measured, not assumed.

\subsubsection{Stress test: where sparsity fails.}
The sparse regime is not a universal property --- so it deserves a controlled
\emph{failure test}. We build a synthetic table (100K rows) with \emph{two
independent, perfectly-correlated column clusters}, $A{=}\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$ and
$B{=}\{b_1,b_2,b_3\}$ (each cluster's columns are equal-valued; the two
clusters are mutually independent), plus an independent filler column $x$.
Candidates are again the 2/3-column subsets. Table \ref{tab:stress} reports the
joint q-error of the best $k{=}1,2,3$ non-overlapping candidates per query.

The contrast is sharp and matches the mechanistic hypothesis
(\S\ref{sec:disc-sparsity}). For single-cluster queries, one dominant 3-column
MCV captures the whole error and adding a second statistic changes nothing
(top-1 $=$ top-2 $=$ top-3 $=1.00$). For the two-cluster query, the best
\emph{single} statistic repairs only one cluster and the other is left to the
independence assumption, so top-1 is still q-error 100; deploying \emph{both}
cluster statistics (top-2) collapses it to 1.00. This is exactly the regime we
flagged as the boundary of our claim in \S\ref{sec:formulation}: sparsity holds
when a query's error is dominated by \emph{one} correlated cluster and fails
when it is driven by \emph{several independent} ones --- in which case the
multi-select model (\S\ref{sec:multi}) is required.

\subsubsection{How sparsity decays with the number of clusters.}
The two-cluster result is not a knife-edge: sparsity degrades smoothly as a
query is driven by more independent clusters. We sweep the number of
\emph{mutually independent} perfectly-correlated clusters $K=1,2,3,4$ (a query
filters on all $K$ clusters), and report the top-1 vs.\ top-$K$ coverage ratio
\[
  \operatorname{cov}_K = \frac{e^0 - e(\{s_1\})}{e^0 - e(\{s_1,\dots,s_K\})},
\]
i.e.\ the fraction of the best $K$-statistic improvement the \emph{single}
best statistic captures (equal to 1 = sparsity holds perfectly).
Table \ref{tab:sweep} shows it is $1.000$ at $K{=}1$, drops to $0.90$ at
$K{=}2$, and collapses to $\approx 0$ once three or more independent clusters
coexist --- exactly the monotone failure regime the mechanistic hypothesis
(\S\ref{sec:disc-sparsity}) predicts, and the regime where the multi-select
model is needed.

This characterization turns ``why sparsity?'' from a hypothesis into a

drive the same query.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the MILP Beat a Budgeted Greedy?}
\label{sec:rq3}

We are careful here, and distinguish two greedy baselines. With a
\emph{generous} budget, the per-query-best greedy \emph{union} (ignoring the
budget) already equals the ILP solution (same 5 statistics, mean 1.021) --- on
a workload of independent single-table queries, greedy is already near-optimal
when nothing constrains it. The interesting question is under \emph{tight}
budgets, where a greedy that must stay within budget spends it on many cheap
statistics and cannot reallocate \emph{capacity}. Table \ref{tab:greedy} compares
the MILP against a budget-respecting greedy (each query's highest

collapse, e.g.\ a 4162 $\to$ 1.4 maximum.

\subsection{RQ4: Does the Approach Scale?}
\label{sec:rq4}

\emph{The optimization is not the bottleneck.} On the full 468-query CENSUS
workload the worst-case (no-prune) MILP has 57{,}735 physical-statistic
variables and 92{,}568 assignment variables (150{,}303 binary variables in
total) and is solved optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.7s; with pruning it
shrinks to 34{,}280 / 46{,}392 / 80{,}672 and solves in $\approx$4.3s.
Measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) dominates the pipeline, not the solve.

\subsection{RQ5: Does Prediction Survive Real PostgreSQL Execution?}
\label{sec:rq5}

We split this into two parts (detailed in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\emph{(a) Are individual candidate measurements predictive?} Yes --- building a
single selected statistic alone reproduces its mask-measured q-error exactly.
\emph{(b) Are co-installed overlapping statistics independent?} No --- when
several overlapping MCVs coexist, the planner can switch a query to a
suboptimal overlapping statistic. This second-order interaction is the one
place where the sparse model's prediction and the real system diverge, and it is
removed by the global-disjointness constraint (\S\ref{sec:p1}).

\subsection{RQ6: Does It Help Where Extended Statistics Cannot?}
\label{sec:rq6}

\textbf{No meaningful benefit --- as a sanity check.} On the join-heavy
\texttt{JOB} workload only 37/113 queries even have a multi-column selection
candidate and, after deduplication, just 7 distinct combinations exist; because
its errors are dominated by joins (which extended statistics cannot directly
repair, \S\ref{sec:mechanisms}), no amount of capacity tuning helps. This is the
expected null result for the negative control: the method does not optimize for
coverage's sake.

\section{End-to-End Validation}
\label{sec:e2e}

We close the loop of the measurement $\to$ optimization $\to$ deployment
pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) by building the ILP-selected sets on the real
database and asking a causal question with three stages: \emph{Is a single
deployed statistic predictable? Is a co-installed overlapping set predictable?
And can we restore predictability?} Each stage controls the previous one, so
together they isolate exactly where the model is sound and where it is not.

\subsection{Stage 1: A Single Deployed Statistic Is Predictable (RQ5a)}
\label{sec:e2e-singleton}

We first confirm that the mask-measured q-error of a single statistic matches
what it delivers when deployed alone. For the statistic
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} the catalog-mask measurement
predicts q-errors of 1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 for queries st.144 / st.588 / st.284.
Building \emph{only} that statistic and re-measuring on the real table gives
1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 --- matching the prediction exactly. This is the
grounding for \S\ref{sec:measure-correct}: per-candidate measurement is sound,
and it validates the economic premise of the whole pipeline (measure each
candidate in isolation, then compose).

\subsection{Stage 2: Co-installed Overlapping Statistics Break Predictability
(RQ5b)}
\label{sec:e2e-coinstalled}

Next we deploy the 2 MB ILP solution for stats\_CEB\_single: five statistics,
all pairwise-overlapping MCVs over \{AnswerCount, FavoriteCount, ViewCount,
PostTypeId\}, predicted mean q-error 1.021. Measured on the real table the mean
is \textbf{1.32} --- a 1.29$\times$ gap. Five of the eight queries match their
prediction (st.562: 1.003, st.308: 1.000, st.182: 1.000, st.398: 1.060, st.314:
1.008), but the three queries served by the \emph{same} statistic
\texttt{(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} degrade from their Stage-1
predictions (1.042/1.029/1.025) to 2.13 / 2.07 / 1.90.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2 for the very same statistic: alone it delivers
1.042; co-installed with four overlapping siblings it gives 2.13.
The \emph{statistic did not change, and its measurement was exact} (Stage 1);
what changed is that the planner now picks \emph{a different} overlapping
statistic for those queries. This is a second-order interaction among
co-installed overlapping statistics --- exactly the independence assumption that
the per-candidate sparse model relies on. It is not a failure to predict a
single statistic; it is a joint effect the per-candidate measurement cannot see
by construction.

\subsection{Stage 3: Global Disjointness Restores Near-Exact Predictability}
\label{sec:p1}

We fix the gap with the Option-A model: a \emph{global-disjointness} constraint
($y_a+y_b\le 1$ whenever the column sets of $a,b$ overlap) forces the deployed
set to be pairwise column-disjoint, so the planner cannot cross-talk. Table
\ref{tab:p1} measures predicted vs E2E for both models at two budgets.

\subsection{The Causal Takeaway}
\label{sec:e2e-takeaway}

The three stages form a clean causal chain. (i) Per-candidate measurement is
sound, and a single deployed statistic is exactly predictable (Stage 1);
(ii) the only place prediction diverges is under co-installed \emph{overlapping}
statistics, where the planner's joint choice violates the per-candidate
independence assumption (Stage 2); and (iii) enforcing pairwise column
disjointness removes that interaction and restores ratio 1.000 (Stage 3), at a
quantified quality cost. This does not undermine the method --- it precisely
delimits where the sparse model holds, and provides a validated, deployable fix.
It is the empirical ``surprise'' that turns validation into a contribution; the
practical alternative when the quality cost of disjointness is unacceptable is
Option B: solve without the constraint, run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check,
drop any interfering statistic, and re-solve.

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Why Does Sparsity Occur? A Mechanistic Hypothesis}
\label{sec:disc-sparsity}

The sparse finding (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) is what makes the whole approach work, so
it deserves a mechanistic account -- though we stress it is a \emph{hypothesis},
not a theorem. Our view is that a single-table selection query's error is
typically dominated by \emph{one} correlated column cluster. Concretely: a
restriction clause is a conjunction of predicates, and its selectivity error is
largest where the columns are most strongly correlated (the independence
assumption fails hardest). The planner's default estimate for such a clause is
about as wrong as its worst correlated sub-clause; a single MCV over exactly
that dominant cluster removes the largest source of error, and residual errors
in the remaining (weakly-correlated) predicates contribute little to the total
q-error. Hence \emph{one} well-chosen statistic captures nearly all improvement
attainable from the 2-/3-column MCV candidates, and a second non-overlapping statistic finds no independent
``second cluster'' worth capturing.

This hypothesis is consistent with every observation we report: it explains why
$k{=}2,3$ add nothing (RQ2), why the top-1 coverage CDF is sharply peaked near
1.0 (P4), why capacity \emph{upgrading} the dominant statistic matters more
than adding others (RQ3), and why the sparse cap costs no quality in the
ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}). It also predicts the boundary of our claim:
workloads where a query is genuinely driven by \emph{several} independent
correlated clusters would violate sparsity and warrant the multi-select model
($\S\ref{sec:formulation}$) instead. Our controlled stress test
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}, Table \ref{tab:stress}) confirms this predicted boundary
directly: a query over two independent clusters goes from top-1 $=$ 100 to
top-2 $=$ 1.00, exactly the many-cluster regime the hypothesis says breaks
sparsity. Characterising how often that regime occurs on real workloads is an
open question (below) and a natural direction for future work.

\subsection{What Is Being Optimized}
\label{sec:disc-objective}

We optimize a \emph{storage} budget (persistent bytes and estimation quality);
ANALYZE maintenance cost is handled separately because PostgreSQL couples its
sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} target on a relation. This keeps the
objective clean and lets us treat capacity as an allocation of persistent
storage rather than a proxy for maintenance cost. The budget-quality curves
(\S\ref{sec:rq3}) and the capacity-level mix (Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}) are
the concrete evidence that a storage budget is, in practice, spent on the
``how much'' axis.

\emph{Why arithmetic mean q-error.} We optimize the arithmetic mean of q-error,
which is sensitive to extreme outliers --- and our workloads \emph{are} outlier
dominated (median stays 1.000 even as the mean drops, \S\ref{sec:rq3}). We
chose it deliberately: q-error is bounded below by 1 with a heavy upper tail,
so the mean rewards repairing the worst queries, which is precisely the cost of
a bad cardinality estimate. We report median/P90/max alongside the mean
everywhere so that outlier-domination is visible rather than hidden (e.g.\ the
CENSUS tail collapse, \S\ref{sec:rq3}). Because the objective is linear in the
$\Delta_{is}$ terms, the arithmetic mean keeps the MILP linear; a geometric
mean (equivalently $\min\sum_i\log e_i$, i.e.\ maximizing
$\sum_s(\log e_i^0-\log e_{is})x_{is}$) would also stay linear while weighting
\emph{relative} rather than \emph{absolute} improvement. We use the arithmetic
mean as the headline and leave the geometric-mean variant as a robustness
question rather than a modeling obstacle.

\subsection{The Interference Fix}
\label{sec:disc-fix}

The validated fix (Option A, global disjointness) is the trustworthy main model:
it restores empirically near-exact predictability at a quantified quality cost
(\S\ref{sec:p1}).
As a practical fallback (Option B), one can solve without the constraint and
then run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check, drop any interfering statistic, and
re-solve. We recommend A as the default because it makes the deployment semantics
match the measurement model (\S\ref{sec:measure-correct}) --- the deployment is
then structurally independent, not just measured independently.

\subsection{Open Questions}

(1) \emph{Level monotonicity}: enforcing that a higher capacity never yields a
worse q-error could prune options and speed the solve, but is not required for
correctness. (2) \emph{Maintenance-aware selection}: jointly modeling ANALYZE
cost (as a function of the \emph{max} target) rather than storage alone is a
natural extension, replacing the storage-only budget with an end-to-end
maintenance budget. (3) \emph{When to upgrade vs.\ add}: our data show a smooth
capacity-only curve for stats\_CEB\_single and a large tail on CENSUS, but a
principled characterisation of the ``upgrade vs.\ add'' decision boundary is
open; a marginal-value heuristic (quality gained per storage unit for an upgrade
vs.\ a new statistic) would make the resource-allocation view of
\S\ref{sec:intro} more precise. (4) \emph{When does sparsity fail?}:
identifying workloads with multiple independent correlated clusters (the regime
where the multi-select model is needed) is the most important open question.
(5) \emph{Limits}: we consider MCV statistics on
single-table workloads; join workloads need other techniques, and the real
system has a limit on the number of statistics per relation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Extended-statistics selection should be treated as a joint \emph{which} and
\emph{how much} decision. Across the single-table workloads we evaluated
(stats\_CEB\_single and the wide CENSUS benchmark) the benefit is strikingly
sparse---one dominant statistic per query---which makes the budget-constrained
problem an exactly-linear MILP solvable in seconds at full-workload scale,
through a catalog-mask protocol that makes per-candidate, per-capacity
measurement feasible on wide tables. End-to-end validation shows per-candidate
prediction is sound and isolates the residual risk to planner cross-talk among
co-installed overlapping statistics; a global-disjointness
constraint restores empirically near-exact predictability. Join-heavy workloads
are the negative
control. The result is a principled, scalable, and honestly-validated recipe
for automating PostgreSQL extended-statistics deployment under a storage
budget.
