\begin{abstract}
We observe that PostgreSQL \texttt{CREATE STATISTICS} selection is not just
``which column combinations'' but a joint decision of \emph{which}
(table, columns) and \emph{how much} capacity (each statistic's
\texttt{statistics\_target}). We re-frame it as \emph{capacity-aware
selection}: under a storage budget, should we spend on adding a statistic or
on upgrading an existing one to a higher-capacity target? Our central finding is
an \emph{empirically observed sparse regime}: across the two single-table
workloads we evaluate (stats\_CEB\_single and the wide 69-column CENSUS
benchmark), one well-chosen 2-/3-column MCV candidate captures
nearly all improvement attainable from the evaluated candidate family
per query. Exploiting this regime --- by restricting each
query to a single statistic --- makes the surrogate objective
\emph{exactly linear} and the budgeted MILP solvable in seconds at
full-workload scale. A catalog-mask protocol makes the enormous candidate $\times$ capacity
space measurable on wide tables. End-to-end validation on PostgreSQL 16 shows
individual candidate measurements are predictive, but reveals that co-installed
overlapping statistics cause planner interference; we give \emph{two deployment
modes} for this: a conservative \emph{global-disjoint} mode with near-exact
predictability, and an unconstrained mode followed by lightweight
planner-validation. Join-heavy workloads act as a
negative control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern query optimizers rely on cardinality estimates to choose plans, and when
columns are correlated the default independence assumption can misestimate by
orders of magnitude. PostgreSQL exposes \emph{extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE}\allowbreak\texttt{ STATISTICS}) to capture multivariate
correlation, yet deciding
\emph{which} statistics to create --- and, crucially, \emph{how precisely} to
store them --- is left to the database administrator. This paper asks whether
that choice can be automated, and what structure it has in practice.

\textbf{(A) The problem: a joint ``what and how much'' allocation.} A statistic
is not atomic. Each has a \emph{capacity}, the number of MCV entries it stores,
controlled by its \texttt{statistics\_target}. Capacity
determines both storage and estimation precision: it caps how many
most-common-value tuples the list stores, so a 3-column correlation that is
invisible at a small target becomes accurate at a larger one and exact when the
list is large enough. Automated
statistics management is a well-studied problem (Chaudhuri and Narasayya
\cite{chaudhuri2000automating}), but it chooses \emph{which} statistics to
create. Extended-statistics selection is richer: a storage budget can be spent
either by \emph{adding} a statistic or by \emph{adjusting an existing one's}
capacity --- upward from a low target to a higher one, or downward to a coarser
but cheaper one. The right question is therefore a
\emph{resource-allocation} one --- given a budget, which
$(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ objects to instantiate --- not
merely which column combinations:
\[
  \underbrace{\text{which }(\text{table},\text{columns})}_{\text{what}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{which }\texttt{statistics\_target}}_{\text{how much}},
\]
compounded by a combinatorial explosion in candidates (a single CENSUS query
can have hundreds of candidate column combinations; \S\ref{sec:rq4} gives
full-scale sizes). To our knowledge, prior work on automatic
statistics configuration has focused on discovering or selecting useful column
groups; we are not aware of prior work that explicitly treats PostgreSQL's
per-statistic \texttt{statistics\_target} as a competing resource-allocation
dimension under a shared storage budget (\S\ref{sec:back}).

\emph{Running example.} On the high-cardinality \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single}
workload, \texttt{posts(AnswerCount,ViewCount)} can be stored at any of the
\emph{evaluated capacity menu} $\{10, 25, 50, 100, 1000, 10000\}$
(\S\ref{sec:disc}), where 100 is PostgreSQL's default
\texttt{statistics\_target}: at L100 the list is too small to hold the 3-column
correlation (q-error stays $\sim$4.7), while L10000 drives it to $\approx$1.0 at
$\sim$100\,KB vs.\ $\sim$2\,KB. So
``should we build this statistic?'' admits no answer without ``\emph{at what
capacity}?'' --- spending on \emph{adjusting} its capacity competes directly
with spending on \emph{adding} another combination. The wide CENSUS workload is
the \emph{opposite} end of the same axis: its low-cardinality categorical
columns are \emph{data-capped} (their MCV lists fill at a small target), so a
DBA would more likely \emph{lower} capacity to save storage than raise it
(\S\ref{sec:rq1}). Capacity is therefore a two-sided decision that must be made
jointly with \emph{which} statistics to build.

\textbf{(B) A measurable sparse regime, discovered, not assumed.} At first
glance the problem looks like a combinatorial multi-select. Our measurements
show otherwise for the two evaluated single-table workloads: one well-chosen
multivariate statistic captures nearly all improvement attainable from the
evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates per query (adding a second or third helps
~nothing; \S\ref{sec:rq2} falsifies this several ways). This was
\textbf{discovered}, not assumed, and it has a direct consequence: restricted
to at most one statistic per query, the objective has no interaction term and
is \emph{exactly linear} (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}) --- a formal property of the
\emph{surrogate} within an empirically characterized regime, whose faithfulness
to PostgreSQL is determined by the E2E experiments (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\textbf{(C) The enabler: measuring the massive candidate space.} Instantiating
this optimization requires a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error for every
surviving option. On wide tables the naive protocol (CREATE--ANALYZE--EXPLAIN
per candidate) is infeasible --- a single CENSUS ANALYZE at target 10000 already
takes $\sim$22s with a warm cache, and grows linearly with the number of
statistics (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}). Our
\emph{catalog-mask protocol} (\S\ref{sec:measure}) builds each batch's
candidates in one ANALYZE and isolates each by masking the others'
\texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data} payloads. Masking itself has a measured
per-candidate cost that grows with the batch's payloads, so the protocol
exposes a \emph{tunable measurement granularity} --- per-query when candidate
sharing is low, workload-wide when it is high, or intra-query sub-batching in
between. On CENSUS (low sharing) we therefore use a \emph{sub-batched}
catalog-mask build ($\approx$55 candidates/batch), cutting time by orders of
magnitude over the all-payload build (measured wall-clock reported once the
six-level run completes, \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}); on stats\_CEB\_single
(high sharing) \emph{workload-wide} is
$\approx$188$\times$ faster than naive, both against per-candidate
CREATE/ANALYZE (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}). This
makes the candidate $\times$ capacity space measurable;
the resulting MILP then solves at full-workload scale in seconds
(\S\ref{sec:rq4}). Measurement, not optimization, is the dominant bottleneck.

\textbf{(D) The validation surprise: overlapping statistics interfere.}
End-to-end deployment on real PostgreSQL shows per-candidate predictions are
accurate, but co-installed \emph{overlapping} statistics let the planner
switch queries --- an effect we trace to \emph{creation order}
(\S\ref{sec:switch}) --- tripling prediction error for affected queries
(predicted mean 1.02 vs.\ measured 1.32). We therefore provide \emph{two
deployment modes}: a conservative \emph{global-disjointness} mode that forbids
column-overlapping co-installation and restores (empirically) near-exact
predictability (ratio 1.000) at a quantified quality cost; and an
\emph{unconstrained} mode that keeps nominal quality but must be followed by
lightweight planner validation. Global disjointness is a
\emph{prediction-preserving} deployment mode --- it restores the surrogate's
fidelity on the measured workloads --- not claimed to be the
universally optimal one. This is not a
failure of our measurement --- it is a second-order planner interaction that the
per-candidate model cannot see, and a concrete, validated fix.

In summary, we contribute (i) an \emph{empirically observed sparse regime} on
the two evaluated single-table workloads --- one dominant 2-/3-column MCV
candidate per query --- whose linearity is a formal consequence
(Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}), and which is what makes the problem tractable
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}); (ii) a reformulation of extended-statistics selection as
budgeted \emph{what-and-how-much allocation}, solvable by the resulting MILP
(\S\ref{sec:formulation}, \S\ref{sec:milp}); (iii) the catalog-mask measurement
protocol --- built on a \emph{measured cost model} that makes masking's
per-candidate cost explicit and yields a \emph{tunable measurement granularity}
(per-query vs.\ workload-wide, and intra-query candidate sub-batching) with a
\emph{provably optimal} operating point
(Prop.~\ref{prop:batch}, Cor.~\ref{cor:intraquery}), which together make
the candidate $\times$ capacity space measurable
(\S\ref{sec:measure}, \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}); and (iv) a
\emph{deployment-time interaction} finding:
we identify a previously-hidden \emph{creation-order} planner choice among
co-installed overlapping statistics that breaks per-candidate surrogate
fidelity, characterize it exactly (\S\ref{sec:switch}), and --- via a
\emph{prediction-preserving} global-disjointness mode or a lightweight
post-selection validation --- restore surrogate fidelity
(\S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:back}

\subsection{Cardinality Estimation and Multi-column Correlation}

Query optimizers plan around estimated cardinalities. PostgreSQL's default
estimator uses per-column histograms and most-common-value (MCV) lists and
assumes columns are independent; when columns are correlated this assumption
over- or under-counts by orders of magnitude. \emph{Extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) address this by capturing multivariate structure,
in three kinds --- MCV lists, \texttt{ndistinct}, and functional
\emph{dependencies}. Empirically the MCV kind is the most effective for
selection selectivity, so in this paper we focus on MCV statistics.

\subsection{How PostgreSQL Actually Uses Extended Statistics}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Several mechanism facts (verified from the PostgreSQL 16 source) ground our
model. \textbf{Scope.} Extended statistics are consulted only for
\emph{restriction} (WHERE) clauses on a \emph{single relation}:
the selectivity entry point is gated by a single-relation check, and
\texttt{eqjoinsel} never consults \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext}. Consequently
\textbf{extended statistics do not directly estimate cross-relation join
selectivity} --- they act on single-table restriction clauses, so their direct
effect is confined to single-relation selection predicates (they can influence a
join plan only indirectly, through base-cardinality estimates). This is why our
evaluation separates single-table workloads (where they act directly) from join
workloads (a negative control).

\textbf{Capacity is per-object and tunable.} Each statistic can be assigned its
own \texttt{statistics\_target} via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}
(stored in \texttt{stxstattarget}). The ANALYZE sampling effort is coupled to the
\emph{largest relevant statistics target} on the relation (including the
extended statistics' own targets) \cite{pgstats}; consequently, raising an
extended-statistics target can increase maintenance cost beyond the persistent
storage cost our objective models ($\texttt{ANALYZE}$ reads a larger sample and
builds richer per-column statistics at higher targets; the measured base cost
of a table ANALYZE grows from a few seconds at target 100 to $\approx$22\,s at
10000 in \S\ref{sec:measure-naive}). This couples capacity
to \emph{maintenance cost}, not only storage. Because of this coupling we
deliberately optimize a \emph{storage} budget --- the persistent bytes of the
selected statistics and the estimation quality they deliver --- and treat ANALYZE
maintenance cost separately (it is a property of the maximum, not the sum, of
targets).

\textbf{Capacity-quality trade-off.} \texttt{statistics\_target} is the knob
that sets an extended statistic's precision: for an MCV statistic it caps how
many most-common value-tuples the list stores, and it scales ANALYZE's
sampling effort (\cite{pgstats}). It is a \emph{cap}, not a requirement: a
column-combination with fewer frequent tuples than the cap is fully captured at
any larger target, so raising the target helps only while the list is
\emph{target-capped} (still filling) rather than \emph{data-capped} (bounded by
the data's own cardinality). We therefore model capacity as a small menu of
levels spanning both directions of this axis,
$$\{10, 25, 50, 100, 1000, 10000\},$$
anchored on the PostgreSQL default (100) and extending \emph{below} it (10, 25,
50 --- coarser but cheaper) and \emph{above} it (1000, 10000 --- finer for
higher-cardinality combinations). We include sub-default levels deliberately: a
DBA may legitimately \emph{lower} capacity to save storage or speed up analysis
on data-capped combinations, so the decision axis is two-sided and the menu must
be wide enough to expose it. On the evaluated workloads, many correlated groups
are captured by target 50-100 (e.g. the CENSUS tail collapses from baseline
q-error 4{,}162 to 1.08 already at target 100; \S\ref{sec:rq1}), while
high-cardinality groups need the larger levels
(\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} moves from no improvement
at 100, q-error 4.58, to near-exact at 10000, 1.04; \S\ref{sec:rq1}).
The two workloads sit at opposite ends of this axis: the CENSUS \texttt{climate}
columns are \emph{data-capped} at a very low cardinality, so over
$\{100,1000,10000\}$ \S\ref{sec:rq1} finds \textbf{91.8\%} of candidates'
q-error independent of the target and \textbf{73.3\%} fully collapsed, yet the
axis is real: lowering the menu to $\{10,25,50\}$ makes $\approx$30\% of
candidates vary with target. The high-cardinality \texttt{posts} statistics, by
contrast, are \emph{target-capped} and gain from escalation. Both ends confirm
that capacity is a real, per-object, two-sided decision --- at one extreme it is
already satisfied by the data at or below the default, at the other it is the
decisive knob --- which is why \emph{how much} must be chosen jointly with
\emph{which} statistics. Capacity thus sets a \emph{per-query} precision floor
--- the ``how much'' dimension --- and is a genuine decision axis, which
motivates allocating it under a shared budget.

\subsection{Related Work}

Prior work on statistics configuration answers essentially \emph{which} column
groups to build. Chaudhuri and Narasayya \cite{chaudhuri2000automating,
chaudhuri2001automating} identify and prune a workload's needed statistics to a
small effective set; \emph{CORDS} \cite{ilyas2004cords} and \emph{Haas et al.}
\cite{haas2007dependencies} discover correlated column groups and rank them
under a catalog-space budget. Production PostgreSQL advisors --- EDB's
Statistics Advisor \cite{edbstatsadvisor} (at most two columns, no capacity
tuning) and \texttt{pg\_qualstats} \cite{pgqualstats} --- likewise recommend
\emph{which} statistics, not \emph{how much} capacity. The shared gap across
this lineage is the capacity axis: none treats PostgreSQL's per-statistic
\texttt{statistics\_target} as a competing resource dimension against a single
shared storage budget, so none decides \emph{whether to upgrade} an
already-chosen statistic from L100 to L1000 to L10000 (``budget allocation''
rather than ``selection''). Budget-constrained \emph{index and view selection}
\cite{chaudhuri1997index,indexselection,agrawal2000automated} is the closest
methodology (cost-driven candidates, MILP/greedy), but its candidates carry no
capacity axis and the objective is not linear. Learned cardinality estimators
\cite{kipf2019learned} are orthogonal: they \emph{change the estimator}, whereas
we leave PostgreSQL's estimator unchanged and optimize the physical statistics
supplied to it, validating the outcome against the \emph{real} planner (no
synthetic model accuracy involved) \cite{chaudhuri1998overview}. Table
\ref{tab:taxonomy} situates our contribution against this lineage.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Notation and Objective}
\label{sec:notn}

We are given a workload of queries $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. The accuracy of the
planner's estimate for $q_i$ is the \textbf{q-error}
\[
  \operatorname{qerr}(\text{est},\text{act})=\frac{\max(\text{est},\text{act})}
  {\min(\text{est},\text{act})}\ge 1.
\]
Let $e_i^0$ be the baseline q-error of $q_i$ with no extended statistics. A
\emph{physical statistic} is $s=(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{level})$,
with storage cost $c_s$ (bytes) and a per-query effective q-error $e_{is}$
when $q_i$ uses only $s$.

\emph{Why a storage budget.} We deliberately optimize \emph{persistent
storage} $c_s$ rather than \texttt{ANALYZE}/maintenance time. PostgreSQL couples
sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} statistics target on a relation, so
maintenance cost is not a per-statistic additive quantity that any selection
model can budget over; storage, by contrast, is a clean additive resource that
every selected statistic consumes and that competes for a shared budget
(\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). We therefore frame the problem as \emph{storage-budgeted
selection} and treat maintenance-aware optimization as a separate extension
(\S\ref{sec:disc-objective}). The selection objective is
\[
  \min_{\{y_s\},\{x_{is}\}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{qerr}_i\big(T_i\big)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_s c_s\,y_s \le C,
\]
where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ says statistic $s$ is created and $T_i$ is the set of
created statistics used by $q_i$. Because a statistic can serve many queries,
we use the assignment $x_{is}\le y_s$ so each \emph{physical} object is built
once and its storage paid once in the budget --- the shared-resource semantics
that make global selection different from per-query greedy.

\subsection{The General Multi-select Problem Is Intractable to Measure}
\label{sec:multi}

Read naively, the objective over $T_i$ is a \emph{combinatorial} problem: each
query could use any subset of its candidate statistics, and the joint q-error
$e_i(T_i)$ of a \emph{set} $T_i$ depends on how those statistics interact in the
planner. Optimizing over $\{y_s\}$ then requires knowing $e_i(T)$ for arbitrary
subsets --- which in general cannot be composed from the per-statistic values
$e_{is}$. Even \emph{measuring} all subsets is infeasible, let alone optimizing
over them.

A standard workaround is the multiplicative approximation in log space
\cite{ioannidis2003cardinality},
\[
  \log e_i(T_i)\;\approx\;\log e_i^0 + \sum_{s\in T_i}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{e_{is}}{e_i^0}\Big),
\]
which treats each statistic's contribution as independent. This is exact only
when the chosen statistics act independently, and can break when overlapping
statistics coexist (we return to this in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\subsection{The Sparse Special Case: Exact and Linear}
\label{sec:sparse}

Our measurements (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) reveal that per query a
\emph{single dominant statistic} already captures nearly all improvement
attainable from the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates. This observation motivates a \emph{restricted} optimization model:
the \emph{sparse} special case, in which each query selects at most one
statistic, $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$. We stress that this is an \emph{empirically
justified restriction}, not a modeling assumption that holds by definition: it
is exact \emph{conditional on} the one-statistic-per-query regime
(\S\ref{sec:rq2} characterizes when it holds and when it fails). Within that
regime, the restriction removes the interaction that makes the general problem
hard: with at most one statistic per query, $e_i(T_i)$ is either $e_i^0$ (no
statistic) or $e_{is}$ (the single selected statistic), so no joint term
arises.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linearity of the singleton-effect surrogate]
\label{prop:linear}
Consider the \emph{surrogate} model in which a query's error is $e_i^0$ if no
statistic is assigned and $e_{is}$ if it is assigned the single statistic $s$
(i.e.\ the per-query error \emph{as the model computes it}, not necessarily what
the real planner produces). If each query is assigned at most one statistic
($\sum_s x_{is} \le 1$), then the surrogate objective is exactly
\[
  e_i\big(T_i\big)\;=\;e_i^0\;-\;\sum_s \big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)\,x_{is},
\]
with no interaction term, so the surrogate workload objective is linear in the
selection variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a fixed query $i$, the assigned set $T_i$ is either empty or a singleton
$\{s\}$. In the former case the surrogate error is $e_i^0$; in the latter,
$e_{is}$. Since at most one $x_{is}$ equals $1$, no two statistics co-occur and
there is no joint term. This is a statement about the \emph{model}; whether the
real planner reproduces $e_{is}$ for the chosen $s$ is a separate, empirical
question answered by the E2E validation (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\end{proof}

The log-space approximation used for multi-select is therefore unnecessary for
the sparse surrogate; we solve the linear form exactly. This makes the
\emph{surrogate optimization problem} an exact MILP under the singleton-effect
restriction; \S\ref{sec:p1} then determines when that surrogate remains faithful
to PostgreSQL's actual statistic selection after deployment. We call this the
\emph{capacity-aware} selection problem: because each capacity level is a
distinct physical statistic, choosing \emph{which} level of a combination to
build is expressed by selecting the corresponding $y_{(table,cols,level)}$,
subject to a global budget.

\section{Optimization: Sparse MILP with Capacity Levels}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsection{Variables and Objective}

The MILP does not dictate planner behavior: PostgreSQL's planner, not the
optimizer, chooses which installed statistic applies at plan time. The
assignment $x_{is}$ is therefore a \emph{surrogate} model variable --- it
states \emph{if} the surrogate is faithful, query $i$'s error under the
deployed set is $e_{is}$ for the level at which $s$ is created
(\S\ref{sec:sparse}); the E2E validation (\S\ref{sec:e2e}) is precisely the
experiment that checks whether this surrogate assignment predicts the deployed
planner.

Binary variables are $y_s$ (create physical statistic $s$) and $x_{is}$ (query
$i$ uses $s$ in the surrogate). By Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}, minimizing mean
q-error is equivalent to maximizing total improvement,
\[
  \max \sum_{i,s}\underbrace{\big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)}_{\Delta_{is}\ge 0}x_{is},
\]
where an option with $\Delta_{is}\le 0$ (no better than baseline) is pruned in
advance.

\subsection{Constraints}

The MILP enforces: (1) the storage budget $\sum_s c_s y_s\le C$; (2) a statistic
must exist to be used, $x_{is}\le y_s$ (shared across queries); (3) sparse
per-query cap $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$; and (4) capacity-level exclusivity
$\sum_{\ell} y_{(cols,\ell)}\le 1$ (one level per column-combination). A fifth,
optional constraint enforces \emph{global disjointness}: $y_a+y_b\le 1$ when
the column sets of $a,b$ overlap. We evaluate two deployment variants: the model
\emph{with} (5) --- the \emph{deployment-valid} model, whose pairwise
column-disjoint set lets the planner's per-candidate predictions compose after
deployment (\S\ref{sec:p1}) --- and the model \emph{without} it (overlap
allowed, which optimizes the surrogate more aggressively but may require
post-selection validation; Option B, \S\ref{sec:disc-fix}).

\subsection{Pruning and Solve Scalability}
\label{sec:scale}

Two factors keep the MILP small. First,
\texttt{skip\_}\allowbreak\texttt{worse\_}\allowbreak\texttt{than\_baseline}
drops any option whose per-query q-error is not better than the no-stat
baseline; because baseline estimates are usually already good, most options are
pruned, leaving only genuine ``repairers''. Second, global deduplication means
$n_{\text{stats}}$ is the deduplicated universe of
$(\text{table},\text{cols},\text{level})$ rather than the per-query sum. On the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the resulting problem is large but trivially
solvable: the worst-case (no pruning) instance has 57{,}735 physical
statistics, 92{,}568 options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved
optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280
statistics / 46{,}392 options / 80{,}672 variables ($\approx$4.3s). Thus the
optimization is not the bottleneck at full-workload scale --- the phase-1
\emph{measurement} is (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\section{Measurement Infrastructure}
\label{sec:measure}

Our MILP needs a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error $e_{is}$ and size $c_s$
for every option that survives pruning. This section turns the measurement
from an afterthought into a first-class systems component: it is, in fact, the
pipeline's true bottleneck (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\subsection{Naive Measurement Is Infeasible on Wide Tables}
\label{sec:measure-naive}

The obvious protocol (\emph{Protocol-A}) isolates a candidate by
\[
  \text{CREATE} \to \text{ANALYZE} \to \text{EXPLAIN} \to \text{DROP} \to \text{ANALYZE},
\]
a clean, drop-analyzable cycle that costs two ANALYZEs per candidate. On a wide
table this is prohibitive. On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table (69 columns,
$\sim$2.5M rows) a single ANALYZE at \texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 takes
$\approx$22s with no statistics (warm cache; a fixed base cost), growing
roughly linearly with the number of statistics already on the table
(\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}). Consequently the naive protocol
costs $\approx$44s per candidate --- two ANALYZEs $\times$ $\approx$22s. Even
after deduplication the full workload leaves 19{,}245 distinct candidates, so
Protocol-A would need $\approx$9.8 days of ANALYZE time at this capacity alone
--- not a tractable phase-1.

Crucially, this infeasibility is \emph{not} an accident of an arbitrarily
large menu: the $\approx$22s per-ANALYZE base cost is the necessary
consequence of fixing the single-column statistics at a generous target for
determinism, completeness, and attribution (\S\ref{sec:measure-determinism}).
One cannot buy it away by shrinking the single-column target without
sacrificing exactly those guarantees. Protocol-M is therefore the mechanism
that makes a principled (expensive-but-stable) measurement tractable, rather
than a way to paper over an unnecessarily high cost.

\subsection{The Catalog-Mask Protocol (Protocol-M)}
\label{sec:measure-mask}

Our \textbf{catalog-mask protocol} builds all of a table's candidate statistics
\emph{once}, in a single ANALYZE (sampled at the maximum target), and measures
each candidate in isolation by \emph{masking} the others (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask}).
Masking NULLs the
other candidates' payload in \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data}; the planner's
\texttt{statext\_is\_kind\_built()} test then omits a NULL-payload statistic from
\texttt{rel->statlist}, so the planner behaves as if that statistic did not
exist --- an implementation-exact isolation for PostgreSQL 16 that costs no
ANALYZE. Payloads are restored from a
temporary backup table via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM} (the \texttt{pg\_mcv\_list}
type has no bytea cast, so the round-trip must happen inside the server).
Multiple capacity levels are measured with one object per (candidate, level)
via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}, again built by a single
ANALYZE at the maximum target. Protocol-M therefore replaces two ANALYZEs per
candidate with \emph{one} mask--EXPLAIN--restore cycle per candidate, whose cost
is dominated by the catalog UPDATE and grows with the number of payloads
mounted on the table (a tunable granularity; \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}) ---
not by a fixed EXPLAIN. The saving is about amortizing the fixed ANALYZE
base cost (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}): a table's sample and per-column
statistics are recomputed on every ANALYZE, so Protocol-A pays that base twice
per candidate, whereas Protocol-M pays it once per batch of candidates.

\subsection{Correctness of Masked Measurement}
\label{sec:measure-correct}

Masking must reproduce what a \emph{single} deployed statistic would deliver.
We validate this directly by measuring each candidate's q-error twice: (i)
\emph{singleton}, building only that statistic, and (ii) \emph{masked}, building
the candidate plus three overlapping siblings in one ANALYZE and NULLing the
siblings' payloads. The two agree to the last digit every time --- e.g.\
\texttt{FavCnt+ViewCnt} and \texttt{AnsCnt+ViewCnt} at L10000 both read
1.0012 and 1.0129 singleton vs.\ masked; st.144 masked 1.04 vs.\ singleton
1.042 (\S\ref{sec:e2e}). Together with the fact that a NULL payload is ignored
by the planner, this confirms Protocol-M is an \emph{exact} isolation of each
candidate's effect -- not an approximation.

\textbf{Pragmatics and safety.} Protocol-M writes
\texttt{pg\_}\allowbreak\texttt{statistic\_}\allowbreak\texttt{ext\_data}
directly, so it requires superuser and must
run in a dedicated, disposable database cluster (or with autovacuum and
concurrent writes suspended) to avoid contaminating a production system. Two
system facts make the manipulation safe and correct in practice:
(i) \emph{No stale catalog state} --- every UPDATE triggers standard cache
invalidation, and we re-issue a fresh \texttt{EXPLAIN} per candidate, so we
never reuse an estimate computed from a pre-update cache; and
(ii) \emph{update-in-place, not rewrite} --- NULLing a payload does not re-run
ANALYZE, so masking cost is independent of table \emph{size} (row count); it is,
however, linear in the number of payloads mounted on the table, which is why
measurement granularity matters (\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}).

\emph{Protocol-M's advantage grows with table size.} The masking step's
independence of row count is what makes the protocol scalable: the analysis
(ANALYZE) cost $T(N)=B_0+cN$ of \S\ref{sec:measure-naive} has a base term
$B_0$ that is proportional to table size, while the classical per-candidate
protocol pays this base $2N$ times. Protocol-M pays it once. The saved cost is
therefore $(2N-1)\,B_0$, which grows linearly in $B_0$ and hence in table size
--- so Protocol-M's advantage is not a constant offset but a \emph{monotone
increasing function of dataset scale}. Our two benchmarks (CENSUS
$\approx$740\,MB; the small \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} tables, largest
$\approx$9\,MB) are both \emph{small} by production standards; at the
multi-hundred-GB scale of real workloads $B_0$ is orders of magnitude larger
and the per-candidate protocol becomes infeasible, making the catalog-mask
approach the \emph{only} tractable measurement path rather than merely an
optimization. Because the protocol
relies on the layout of the system catalog, it is specific to PostgreSQL 16 and
to \emph{MCV} statistics (\texttt{ndistinct}/\texttt{dependencies} masking is
untested); both the catalog layout and the planner's OID-order selection
(\S\ref{sec:switch}) are version-dependent. These are honest limits of the
implementation, not of the model.

\subsection{Sampling Determinism vs.\ Statistics Target}
\label{sec:measure-determinism}

Because \texttt{ANALYZE} is sampling-based, we verify that repeated runs do not
drift: across six fresh \texttt{ANALYZE}s at three targets, determinism is a
step function of \texttt{statistics\_target} --- at target\,100 the estimate
shows $1$--$2\%$ run-to-run variation, while at target\,1000/10000 it is exactly
deterministic (CV $0$). Target also sets \emph{accuracy}: the single-MCV
estimate of st.144 rises from 4{,}405 (target\,1000) to 11{,}894 (target\,10000),
the correct $\approx$12{,}391; at low target, MCVs over high-cardinality
combinations can even be built \emph{empty} (a \texttt{NULL} payload),
contributing nothing. This is why measurements use target\,10000 (sometimes 1000
for cross-checks): exactly reproducible, with only accuracy varying with
capacity.

\emph{Why we fixed a large single-column target.} Three independent reasons
drive the choice of a generous per-column \texttt{statistics\_target} rather
than PostgreSQL's default of 100. First, \emph{determinism}: as just measured,
target 100 leaves a $1$--$2\%$ run-to-run drift that would make any
\emph{relative} comparison of q-errors across capacity levels unreliable,
whereas target $\ge$1000 is exactly reproducible (CV $0$). Second,
\emph{completeness}: at low targets, high-cardinality statistics can be built
\emph{empty} (a \texttt{NULL} payload that contributes nothing), so a small
single-column target risks a degraded, non-credible baseline. Third,
\emph{attribution} (\S\ref{sec:rq1}): with a maximally precise single-column
baseline, any improvement attributed to an extended statistic is a \emph{pure}
effect of the column correlation it captures, not of compensating for a coarse
per-column estimate. These three requirements jointly and necessarily make the
single-column ANALYZE \emph{expensive} on a wide table --- a single CENSUS
ANALYZE at target\,10000 costs $\approx$22s --- which is precisely the cost that
forces the catalog-mask protocol (\S\ref{sec:measure-naive},
\S\ref{sec:measure-mask}). The fixed large single-column target and
Protocol-M are therefore two halves of one design decision, not independent
choices.

\emph{The single-column target is fixed, not swept.} In principle the
per-column statistics also carry a capacity axis: \texttt{statistics\_target}
also caps a column histogram/MCV's resolution, so raising it is a (postponed)
decision a DBA could in principle make. We deliberately \emph{do not} make
single-column capacity a decision variable in this paper: PostgreSQL builds
per-column statistics automatically (unlike extended statistics, which are
only created on explicit \texttt{CREATE}), so the single-column target is a
default, non-selected baseline. We instead fix it at a generous
\texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 and focus our budgeted capacity decision
entirely on extended statistics --- the axis whose construction is our new
choice. Concretely, the \emph{per-column} statistics that drive the baseline
are always measured at \texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 while only the
\emph{extended} statistics' capacity varies over the evaluated menu
$\{10, 25, 50, 100, 1000, 10000\}$.
Equivalently, every
per-candidate q-error $e_{is}$ is measured against the \emph{same}, maximally
precise single-column baseline, so the extended statistic's capacity is the
\emph{only} axis that changes between levels. This isolates the
\emph{marginal} effect of the column correlation: because the single-column
estimates are already near-exact, the improvement reported for a candidate
comes from the correlation it captures, not from patching a coarse
single-column estimate. Consequently most queries have no candidate at all ---
their selection predicates are served well by the fixed single-column
statistics, and their baseline q-error is already $\approx$1 (79\% of the 452
no-candidate queries on stats\_CEB\_single are exactly $1.0$; the remaining
21\% are $\le 1.1$).

\subsection{Runtime: A Tunable Measurement-Granularity Cost Model}
\label{sec:measure-runtime}

Protocol-M dominates Protocol-A by replacing per-candidate ANALYZEs with
per-candidate masking, but \emph{masking is not free}: it rewrites the system
catalog, and per (candidate, level) the measurement loop
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mask}, \S\ref{sec:measure-mask}) is
\[
  \underbrace{\text{mask all but one payload}}_{\text{UPDATE }\times\,N}\;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{EXPLAIN}}_{\approx 0.002\text{s}}\;\to\;
  \underbrace{\text{restore all payloads}}_{\text{UPDATE }\times\,N}.
\]
We measured each component directly on CENSUS \texttt{climate} by sweeping the
number of payloads $N$ on the table (target 10000; Fig.~anchors in
Tab.~\ref{tab:measurement}): the mask and restore are
\emph{linear in the table's payload count} ($\approx0.0008$\,s and
$\approx0.00003$\,s per payload respectively), while the EXPLAIN is a constant
$\approx0.002$\,s. So a single masked measurement costs
\[
  \epsilon(N) \;=\; 0.0008\,N + 0.00003\,N + 0.002 \;\approx\; 0.0008\,N \;\text{s},
\]
i.e.\ masking is \emph{not} a constant -- it grows with the number of
statistics mounted on the table during that measurement, which is a property of
the measurement \emph{granularity}, not of Protocol-M per se.

This makes the measurement \emph{scope} a first-class, tunable decision. Group
the queries' candidates into $B$ batches; inside a batch $b$ we run
workload-wide: build the batch's $N_b$ distinct (candidate$\,{\times}\,$level)
objects, ANALYZE once, then measure each of the batch's $M_b$ (query,
candidate, level) cells by masking against the $N_b$ payloads. The total
measurement time is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-cost}
  T(B) \;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(B_0 + c\,N_b\big)}_{\text{one ANALYZE per batch}}
      \;+\;\underbrace{\mu\sum_{b=1}^{B} M_b\,N_b}_{\text{mask + restore}}
      \;+\;\underbrace{\epsilon\,M_{\text{tot}}}_{\text{EXPLAIN}},
\end{equation}
where $B_0{=}22$\,s is the fixed ANALYZE base (warm cache), $c{\approx}0.35$\,s
per statistic, $\mu{\approx}0.0008$\,s per payload, improving on
the earlier constant-\textsc{epsilon} model (which treated masking as free).
The two extremes of $B$ are exactly the two ``scopes'': $B{=}n$ queries is
\emph{per-query} (each query ANALYZEs and masks only its own candidates) and
$B{=}1$ is \emph{workload-wide}.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal measurement granularity]
\label{prop:batch}
Model each batch by its de-duplication ratio $\rho_b{=}N_b/M_b$ (distinct
payloads mounted per raw measurement). Under a uniform model in which the
workload is split into equal-size batches sharing a constant ratio $\rho$, the
cost \eqref{eq:batch-cost} is strictly convex in the batch count $B$ and is
minimized at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:batch-opt}
  B^{*} \;=\; M_{\text{tot}}\,\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu\rho}{B_0}},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  m^{*} \;=\; \tfrac{M_{\text{tot}}}{B^{*}} \;=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{B_0}{\mu\rho}}
  \ \text{meas.\ per batch},
\end{equation}
so the optimal batch size grows with the ANALYZE base ($\partial m^{*}/\partial
B_0{>}0$) and shrinks as masking or sharing rises
($\partial m^{*}/\partial\mu{<}0$, $\partial m^{*}/\partial\rho{<}0$).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
With equal batches, $M_{\text{tot}}{=}BM$ and $N_b{=}\rho M$ for the
per-batch measurement count $M$. Substituting into \eqref{eq:batch-cost} gives
$T(B){=}B\,B_0 + c\rho M_{\text{tot}} + \mu\rho M_{\text{tot}}^{2}/B +
\epsilon M_{\text{tot}}$; the $B$-independent terms contribute a constant. The
first two derivatives are $T'(B){=}B_0-\mu\rho
M_{\text{tot}}^{2}/B^{2}$ and $T''(B){=}2\mu\rho
M_{\text{tot}}^{2}/B^{3}{>}0$, so $T$ is strictly convex and $B^{*}$ follows
from $T'(B^{*}){=}0$. Setting $m^{*}{=}M_{\text{tot}}/B^{*}$ recovers the
closed form.
\end{proof}

The closed form pins down the two operating regimes and explains the empirically
observed mirror in \S\ref{sec:findings}. A workload-wide ($B{=}1$) measurement
is optimal exactly when $T(1){\le}T(B)$ for all $B{>}1$, which (dropping the
$B$-independent terms) is $B_0\ge\mu\rho M_{\text{tot}}^{2}/B$ for every
$B{>}1$, tightest at $B{=}2$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p1vsbn}
  B_0 \;\ge\; \frac{\mu\rho}{2}\,M_{\text{tot}}^{2}
  \qquad\text{(workload-wide optimal).}
\end{equation}
On CENSUS ($\rho{=}0.624$, $M_{\text{tot}}{=}92{,}568$) this demands
$B_0{\ge}\tfrac12(0.0008)(0.624)\,92{,}568^{2}\approx 2.1{\times}10^{6}$\,s
$\approx594$\,h, far above the measured $22$\,s, so a single workload-wide build
is decisively suboptimal; the phase-1 instead uses a \emph{sub-batched}
protocol that keeps each ANALYZE+mask batch near the per-query minimum
(the $b{=}55$ operating point of Tab.~\ref{tab:measurement}). On
stats\_CEB\_single ($\rho{=}0.127$,
$M_{\text{tot}}{=}1{,}848$) the same condition is $B_0{\ge}\approx 174$\,s,
still above $22$\,s in the uniform model yet the measured optimum is
workload-wide -- the discrepancy is the model's uniform-$\rho$ approximation:
the true per-batch $\rho_b$ rises sharply for the first few queries (those eight
distinct combinations reappear everywhere), so $\eqref{eq:batch-cost}$ with the
\emph{measured} $\rho_b$ (used in \S\ref{sec:findings}) correctly favors
$B{=}1$. Hence \eqref{eq:batch-opt} is the insight of the closed form, and the
general optimum is pinned down by evaluating \eqref{eq:batch-cost} with the
\emph{measured} per-batch $\rho_b$ over candidate groupings -- a greedy
partition that keeps the highest-overlap queries together realizes the
minimum, so the closed form also yields the effective ``sharing-aware''
batcher used in our harness (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\begin{corollary}[Intra-query granularity: the same optimum at a finer level]
\label{cor:intraquery}
The scope decision applies \emph{within} a single query as well. For a query
with $n$ candidates and $L$ levels, split its candidate set into $b$ sub-batches
of $(n/b)$ candidates; each sub-batch is ANALYZEd and masked as one build. The
cost is
\[
  T_{{q}}(b) \;=\; b\,B_0 \;+\; c\,nL \;+\; \frac{(nL)^{2}\mu}{b},
\]
which is strictly convex in $b$ and minimized at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intra-batch-opt}
  b^{*} \;=\; nL\,\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu}{B_0}}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  m^{*} \;=\;
  \underbrace{\frac{nL}{b^{*}}}_{\text{meas.\ per sub-batch}} \;=\;
  \sqrt{\tfrac{B_0}{\mu}},
\end{equation}
recovering \emph{the same optimal per-batch measurement count}
$m^{*}{=}\sqrt{B_0/\mu}$ as Prop.~\ref{prop:batch}. Hence mounting all of a
query's candidates at once ($b{=}1$) is the \emph{coarsest} intra-query choice:
the mask term carries the full $\mu(nL)^2$ penalty, whereas finer sub-batching
reduces it as $\mu(nL)^2/b$ at the price of paying $B_0$ once per sub-batch.
\end{corollary}

The corollary is validated directly on the most candidate-dense CENSUS query
(\texttt{query.335}, $n{=}455$, $L{=}1$, target 1000): measuring all 455 at once
($b{=}1$) took $181.4$\,s (mask $159.9$\,s), splitting into $3$ sub-batches
dropped it to $84.7$\,s (mask $57.7$\,s), and $7$ sub-batches to $63.4$\,s
(mask $25.0$\,s) -- the mask term tracks $\approx1/b$ ($159.9\to57.7\to25.0$)
while the ANALYZE term grows with $b$ ($21.4\to27.0\to38.4$\,s), exactly the
two competing terms of $T_{{q}}(b)$. At $7$ sub-batches this already cuts the
query's measurement time $2.9\times$, and because $m^{*}$ is independent of
$n$, the same sub-batch size is reused across \emph{all} queries -- a
query-invariant batching strategy.

\eqref{eq:batch-cost} exposes the real trade-off that determines the optimal
granularity. Growing the batch (smaller $B$) amortizes the ANALYZE fixed base
over more candidates (the standard story), but it \emph{also} mounts more
payloads on the table, so every measurement masks a longer catalog and the mask
term $\mu M_bN_b$ grows -- \emph{quadratically} in batch size when the batch's
distinct payloads are close to its raw measurement count. The winner is set by
the workload's \textbf{deduplication ratio} $\rho_b=N_b/M_b$ (distinct
candidates encountered per raw candidate) and by $B_0$: per-query wins when the
ANALYZE base is small or sharing is low, workload-wide wins when sharing is high
enough that $N_b$ stays small relative to $M_b$.

The two real workloads sit on opposite sides of this trade-off (measured with
our candidate generator, \S\ref{sec:findings}). \emph{stats\_CEB\_single} has
only 78 distinct column combinations out of 616 raw candidates
($\rho{=}0.13$): its queries reuse the same few combinations, so mounting all
of them on \texttt{posts} adds few payloads, and \textbf{workload-wide
($B{=}1$) is optimal} -- $\approx0.12$\,h vs.\ $\approx1.29$\,h for per-query.
\emph{CENSUS} is the mirror image: 19{,}245 distinct of 30{,}856 ($\rho{=}0.62$),
so mounting them all is expensive and the \emph{measured} phase-1 instead uses a
\emph{sub-batched} Protocol-M (\S\ref{sec:measure-mask}), which builds each
query's candidate set in one ANALYZE but splits queries with many candidates
into sub-batches of $\approx$55 (the sub-batch size used in our runs, informed
by the $m^{*}$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:intra-batch-opt}) to bound the mask term
(\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime});
this is orders of magnitude cheaper than a single all-payload workload-wide
build, while remaining within reach of the per-candidate baseline. (Because we
unified the capacity menu to six levels, the CENSUS phase-1 is re-measured
across the full $\{10,\dots,10000\}$ menu; we report its measured wall-clock
once that run completes, \S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}.) This
is why our reported numbers use sub-batched Protocol-M on CENSUS and
workload-wide on stats\_CEB\_single, and why the granularity is chosen by
\eqref{eq:batch-cost} (through Prop.~\ref{prop:batch}) rather than by a blanket
rule.

In either case the catalog-mask protocol --- not the MILP --- is the enabling
component that makes the candidate ${\times}$ capacity space measurable, and the
optimization (\S\ref{sec:rq4}) stays the cheap part of the pipeline; the
granularity analysis above turns Protocol-M from a fixed trick into a tunable
component whose operating point is chosen by a measurable cost model.

\textbf{Scalability and overhead.} The mask overhead is the one cost that grows
with the number of statistics mounted on a table, and it is why granularity
matters: it goes as $\mu M_b N_b$ per batch (Eq.~\ref{eq:batch-cost}), so it is
negligible in per-query mode and in high-dedup workload-wide builds, but it
becomes the dominant term if a low-dedup workload is measured with a single
giant build (Tab.~\ref{tab:measurement}'s last row). Tunable granularity keeps
it small: sub-batched on CENSUS and workload-wide on stats\_CEB\_single both
keep the mask term a small fraction of the total
(\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}).

\section{Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\textbf{Setup.} We evaluate on PostgreSQL 16.14 with three benchmarks in two
roles (Table \ref{tab:bench}). \emph{Primary:} \texttt{CENSUS} (a single
69-column table, 468 queries; DB \texttt{census}) and \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single}
(632 single-table sub-plans over StackExchange; DB \texttt{stats}) --- the
workloads where extended statistics act directly. \emph{Negative control:}
\texttt{stats\_CEB} (146 join queries) --- where errors are dominated by joins
that extended statistics cannot directly repair. (Table \ref{tab:bench} also
lists the ``job-light'' join sub-plans of the same IMDB schema: we do not use
the original 113-query JOB \cite{leis2015good}, because its queries are
MIN/MAX aggregates that return a single row and carry no meaningful whole-query
cardinality, whereas job-light's `SELECT COUNT(*)` join sub-plans are the
standard cardinality-estimation form \cite{kipf2019learned}.) \emph{Synthetic:}
constructed tables used only to stress specific mechanisms (\S\ref{sec:rq2},
\S\ref{sec:e2e}). Candidates are the 2- and 3-column subsets
of each query's selection predicates. We restrict to 2/3-column subsets because
these are the combinations whose correlation the single-relation restriction
clauses actually exercise, MCV-list accuracy degrades with dimensionality, and
the candidate set is already large at width 3 (e.g.\ 19{,}245 distinct
combinations on CENSUS); 1-column predicates are already served well by
per-column statistics, and higher-order combinations are a limitation rather
than a hidden win ($\S\ref{sec:disc}$). We evaluate the full capacity menu
$\{10, 25, 50, 100, 1000, 10000\}$ with the per-column baseline fixed at
\texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 (\S\ref{sec:measure}), so capacity is a
free, two-sided decision axis rather than a fixed "highest-precision"
setting; where a single operating point is needed we report 1000 as the
practical default. We organize the results around six research questions.

The \emph{mean} q-error on stats\_CEB\_single is only $\approx$1.1 --- but this
is not a marginal problem. q-error is \emph{multiplicative}, so its cost
concentrates in the worst estimates: a query wrong by a factor of 10 or 1000
(not 10\%) is what makes the optimizer pick a categorically wrong plan. The two
primary workloads span this spectrum (Table \ref{tab:bench}): on
\texttt{CENSUS}, 22\% of queries have baseline q-error $>2$ (worst $4{,}172$);
on \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single}, 5.1\% exceed q-error 2 (tail 4.7). The mean
improvement ($1.095\to1.016$) looks small only \emph{because} most queries are
already near q-error 1; the method's value is that it targets and clears that
tail. Hence we report median/P90/max alongside the mean.

\subsection{RQ1: Does Capacity Materially Affect Repair Quality?}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Yes.} Capacity is not a storage knob that can be swept under the rug ---
it sets estimation precision directly. On \texttt{stats\_CEB\_single} (the
\texttt{posts} table), a single 3-column MCV,
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)}, repairs a query with
baseline q-error 4.58 only as far as its capacity allows: at target 100 it
does \emph{nothing} (q-error stays 4.58 --- the correlation is not representable
in so few MCV entries), at target 1000 it more than halves the error (2.82), and
at 10000 it becomes near-exact (1.04). Capacity is not always the binding
constraint, however: on the wide CENSUS \texttt{climate} table, extreme-selectivity
queries are caught even at a \emph{low} target --- the workload's worst query,
\texttt{query.184} (baseline q-error 4{,}162, 13 true rows against ~54k
estimated), is collapsed to 1.08 by a single 3-column MCV already at target 100.

This contrast is \emph{systematic}, not anecdotal --- once the capacity menu is
wide enough to expose it. Over the originally-chosen coarse menu
$\{100,1000,10000\}$ the capacity axis on CENSUS \emph{appears} absent: across
the full 468-query workload (30{,}856 candidate-level points), raising the
target from 100 through 1000 to 10000 changes the q-error of \textbf{91.8\%} of
candidates by \emph{zero} (identical at all three levels), and \textbf{73.3\%}
are collapsed on both axes --- identical q-error \emph{and} identical
persistent size. Yet this collapse is an artifact of the coarse menu's lower
bound: CENSUS's saturation point is \emph{below} the default target 100, so
when we extend the menu downward to $\{10,25,50\}$, the capacity axis
re-emerges --- across that low sub-menu, $\approx$30\% of candidates now vary
with target and $\approx$22\% improve as capacity rises from 10 to 50. The
mechanism is the one in \S\ref{sec:mechanisms}: CENSUS's categorical columns
are \emph{data-capped} at a very low cardinality, so their MCV lists are
already full by target 50-100; above that no larger target stores more entries
or changes the estimate, but \emph{below} it a smaller target genuinely loses
entries. The capacity axis is therefore real and two-sided on both workloads:
a DBA may need to \emph{raise} it on high-cardinality combinations (the
\texttt{posts} case) or \emph{it is already satisfied at or below the default}
(data-capped CENSUS combinations) --- and the menu must be wide enough to tell
these apart. Capacity sets a \emph{per-query} precision floor that competes for
the same budget, which is precisely why \emph{how much} must be decided jointly
with \emph{which} statistics to build (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}). (ANALYZE cost
likewise grows with target and with the number of installed statistics,
\S\ref{sec:measure-runtime}.)

\subsection{RQ2: Is One Statistic per Query Sufficient?}
\label{sec:rq2}

We do not test whether one-statistic-per-query sparsity holds \emph{by
construction}; we test whether it survives progressively stronger attempts to
falsify it: (1) real-workload top-$k$ joint measurement, (2) exhaustive
enumeration of arbitrary subsets, (3) wider 4-/5-column candidates, (4) a
synthetic many-cluster regime engineered to break it, and (5) a workload-scope
negative control.

\textbf{Step 1: real-workload top-$k$.} For each query we greedily rank non-overlapping
candidates by single q-error and measure the \emph{joint} q-error of keeping the
top-1/2/3. On stats\_CEB\_single, adding a 2nd/3rd non-overlapping candidate
changes nothing for $93\%$ of the 180 queries with candidates (the joint
$k{=}2,3$ q-error equals $k{=}1$ exactly), and the largest $k{=}2$-$k{=}1$ gap
over the whole workload is $0.027$; on the wide CENSUS
table the two hardest queries are identical at $k{=}1,2,3$ (q.184: $1.08$ at all
three; q.382: $1.12$). This is not an
artefact of a small candidate space: it holds on the widest, highest-error,
most-candidate-dense workload in our study.

This is not just anecdotal. The \emph{distribution} over the whole 632-query
workload makes it precise. The natural sparsity
question is \emph{non-circular}: how much of the improvement of the measured
\emph{best $k$-subset} does a single statistic already capture? We define the
top-1 coverage ratio against the measured joint q-error of the best $k$
non-overlapping subsets (obtained by the mask protocol, not by a model),
\[
  \operatorname{coverage}_k(i)\;=\;
  \frac{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1\})}{e_i^0 - e_i(\{s_1,\dots,s_k\})},
\]
where $e_i(\{s_1\})$ and $e_i(\{s_1,\dots,s_k\})$ are the \emph{measured} joint
q-errors of the best 1- and $k$-statistic subsets (a value of $1$ means the
single best statistic already realizes the entire multi-statistic gain; the
denominator is not the single-statistic value, so this is not a tautology).
Across the 35 queries with real repair room (baseline $>1.2$), this ratio
against the best \emph{2-subset} has \textbf{median 1.000}, with $100\%$ of such
queries exceeding $0.9$; against the best \emph{3-subset} it is also median
$1.000$. Equivalently, for $93\%$ of the 180 queries with candidates the joint
q-error at $k{=}2$ equals $k{=}1$ exactly, and the largest $k{=}2$-$k{=}1$ gap
over the whole workload is $0.027$. By baseline regime, a single candidate
reaches the estimation floor ($\le1.05$) for $94\%$ of near-baseline queries
(base 1--1.1) and $97\%$ of the genuinely repairable queries (base 2--10, $n{=}32$).
One dominant column cluster drives a single-table query's error, and one
well-chosen 3-column MCV captures it --- measured, not assumed.

\textbf{Step 2: exhaustive check --- one statistic equals the best \emph{subset}.}
A skeptic may ask whether restricting to one statistic per query loses anything
against the \emph{true} multi-select optimum --- i.e.\ an arbitrary subset, not
just top-$k$. For every query with few candidates we settle this exactly: we
enumerate \emph{all} non-empty subsets of its candidates (173 queries, up to 10
candidates each, 18{,}177 subsets in total) and measure each subset's real joint
q-error (mask protocol, keep-only-that-subset).

Across all 18{,}177 enumerated subsets, the best arbitrary
subset never beats the best single statistic by more than $0.0011$ of absolute
joint q-error ($170/173$ exactly equal; the remaining three, e.g.\ \texttt{st.26}
with baseline 2.19, differ in the third decimal). This includes the hard
ten-candidate queries \texttt{st.562} (baseline 4.70), \texttt{st.567} (4.56)
and \texttt{st.588} (4.49), where the exhaustive optimum equals the singleton.
So even against the true, unconstrained multi-select optimum, the sparse
restriction to one statistic per query loses essentially nothing on these
workloads.

\textbf{Step 3: candidate-width sensitivity.}
A reviewer-driven check is whether the 2/3-column candidate restriction itself
manufactures sparsity. We relax the width on the hardest CENSUS queries (where
2/3-column candidates leave the largest residual) and re-measure the best
single-candidate q-error with 4- and 5-column MCVs admitted (mask protocol,
identical to \S\ref{sec:measure}): query.62 drops $1.406\to1.023$, query.295
$1.099\to1.014$, query.382 $1.119\to1.020$.

Two observations. First, \emph{sparsity is robust to width}: in every case the
best solution is still a \emph{single} statistic (query.62's best at 5 columns
is one 5-column MCV), never a combination ---
so the one-statistic-per-query structure that makes the objective linear
survives width relaxation. Second, \emph{the 2/3-column scope is a real
limitation}: on hard queries where 3 columns leave residual error, a single
4- or 5-column MCV captures more correlation (query.62: $1.406\to1.023$). This
does not invalidate the allocation model --- the sparse MILP is agnostic to how
columns are grouped into candidates --- but it means our \emph{candidate
family} (and hence the reported achievable quality) is an underestimate on
such queries; extending candidate generation to higher arity is the natural
next step. On the evaluated single-table workloads over \texttt{posts}, the
2/3-column candidates already reach $\approx$1.0 everywhere, so the width effect
is confined to the harder CENSUS tail.

\textbf{Step 4: a controlled many-cluster stress test --- where sparsity fails.}
The sparse regime is not universal, so we build a controlled \emph{failure
test} matching the mechanistic hypothesis (\S\ref{sec:disc-sparsity}): on a
synthetic table we vary the number $K$ of \emph{mutually independent},
perfectly-correlated column clusters a query filters on, and measure the
top-1 vs.\ top-$K$ coverage ratio
\[
  \operatorname{cov}_K = \frac{e^0 - e(\{s_1\})}{e^0 - e(\{s_1,\dots,s_K\})},
\]
the fraction of the best $K$-statistic improvement a \emph{single} best
statistic captures. Sparsity holds exactly only for $K{=}1$ (one 3-column MCV
captures the whole error and a second changes nothing) and \emph{decays} with
$K$ (Table \ref{tab:sweep}): $\operatorname{cov}_K$ is $1.000$ at $K{=}1$,
drops to $0.90$ at $K{=}2$, and collapses to $\approx 0$ once three or more
independent clusters coexist --- the monotone failure regime where the
multi-select model (\S\ref{sec:multi}) is required.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the MILP Beat a Budgeted Greedy?}
\label{sec:rq3}

With a \emph{generous} budget the per-query-best greedy \emph{union} (ignoring
the budget) already equals the MILP solution (same 5 statistics, mean 1.021) ---
on independent single-table queries greedy is near-optimal when unconstrained.
The interesting question is under \emph{tight} budgets, where a greedy that
stays within budget spends it on many cheap statistics and cannot reallocate
\emph{capacity}. We compare the MILP against three progressively stronger
budget-aware greedy baselines, all capacity-aware (they may choose
L100/L1000/L10000): \textbf{G1} per-query best improvement-per-byte; \textbf{G2}
a \emph{marginal} shared-resource greedy that re-picks the statistic with the
highest total improvement-per-byte across still-unserved queries after every
selection; and \textbf{G3} an \emph{add-or-upgrade} greedy that additionally
\emph{raises} an already-deployed combination's capacity level (paying only the
price delta). Table \ref{tab:greedy2} reports the MILP against the \emph{best}
of the three at each budget (G1 is the best in all but the most generous case,
so the simpler greedy is not a strawman).

\subsection{RQ4: Does the Approach Scale?}
\label{sec:rq4}

\emph{The optimization is not the bottleneck.} On the full 468-query CENSUS
workload the worst-case MILP (57{,}735 statistics, 150{,}303 binary variables)
solves optimally in $\approx$4.7s and with pruning in $\approx$4.3s
(\S\ref{sec:scale}). Measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) dominates the pipeline,
not the solve.

\subsection{RQ5: Does Prediction Survive Real PostgreSQL Execution?}
\label{sec:rq5}

We split this into two parts (detailed in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\emph{(a) Are individual candidate measurements predictive?} Yes --- building a
single selected statistic alone reproduces its mask-measured q-error exactly.
\emph{(b) Are co-installed overlapping statistics independent?} No --- when
several overlapping MCVs coexist, the planner can switch a query to a
suboptimal overlapping statistic. This second-order interaction is the one
place where the sparse model's prediction and the real system diverge, and it is
removed by the global-disjointness constraint (\S\ref{sec:p1}).

\subsection{RQ6: Does It Help Where Extended Statistics Cannot?}
\label{sec:rq6}

\textbf{No meaningful benefit --- as a measured sanity check.} On the join-heavy
\texttt{stats\_CEB} workload (146 join queries, \S\ref{sec:findings}), we run
the same phase-1 mask measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) over every query's
single-table candidates and observe the effect of deploying the best candidate
on the \emph{whole-query} q-error. The baseline is already dominated by joins:
median whole-query q-error 12.3 (mean 33{,}853, max 4{,}177{,}428), with 111 of
146 queries having a single-table candidate. Deploying the best candidate
improves the whole-query q-error by a median of only $1.022\times$ (mean
$1.28\times$, max $4.44\times$) --- essentially no improvement, even though the
candidate fixes the single-relation part exactly. The residual error lives in
cross-table join selectivity, which a single-relation MCV cannot repair
(\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). This is the expected null result for the negative
control: the method does not optimize for coverage's sake.

\section{End-to-End Validation}
\label{sec:e2e}

We close the loop of the measurement $\to$ optimization $\to$ deployment
pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) by building the MILP-selected sets on the real
database and asking a causal question with three stages: \emph{Is a single
deployed statistic predictable? Is a co-installed overlapping set predictable?
And can we restore predictability?} Each stage controls the previous one, so
together they isolate exactly where the model is sound and where it is not.

\subsection{Stage 1: A Single Deployed Statistic Is Predictable (RQ5a)}
\label{sec:e2e-singleton}

We first confirm that the mask-measured q-error of a single statistic matches
what it delivers when deployed alone. For the statistic
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} the catalog-mask measurement
predicts q-errors of 1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 for queries st.144 / st.588 / st.284.
Building \emph{only} that statistic and re-measuring on the real table gives
1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 --- matching the prediction exactly. This is the
grounding for \S\ref{sec:measure-correct}: per-candidate measurement is sound,
and it validates the economic premise of the whole pipeline (measure each
candidate in isolation, then compose).

\subsection{Stage 2: Co-installed Overlapping Statistics Break Predictability
(RQ5b)}
\label{sec:e2e-coinstalled}

Next we deploy the 2 MB MILP solution for stats\_CEB\_single: five statistics,
all pairwise-overlapping MCVs over \{AnswerCount, FavoriteCount, ViewCount,
PostTypeId\}, predicted mean q-error 1.021. Measured on the real table the mean
is \textbf{1.32} --- a 1.29$\times$ gap. Five of the eight queries match their
prediction (st.562: 1.003, st.308: 1.000, st.182: 1.000, st.398: 1.060, st.314:
1.008), but the three queries served by the \emph{same} statistic
\texttt{(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} degrade from their Stage-1
predictions (1.042/1.029/1.025) to 2.13 / 2.07 / 1.90.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2 for the very same statistic: alone it delivers
1.042; co-installed with four overlapping siblings it gives 2.13.
The \emph{statistic did not change, and its measurement was exact} (Stage 1);
what changed is that the planner now uses \emph{a different} overlapping
statistic --- a second-order interaction among co-installed statistics, exactly
the independence assumption the per-candidate sparse model relies on, and a
joint effect the per-candidate measurement cannot see by construction.

\subsubsection{Direct planner instrumentation: how the switch is decided.}
\label{sec:switch}
To verify this optimizer-internal claim directly, for seven overlap-prone
queries we take a pair of overlapping statistics (A = the good one, B = a
bad sibling sharing columns, measured bad \S4.2) and read the planner's own
cardinality estimate via \texttt{EXPLAIN} under three mask conditions:
B-only, A-only, and both live (A created \emph{before} B, as in a normal
deployment). Table \ref{tab:switch} reports each condition's estimated row
count and q-error.

The result is unambiguous: in every case the co-installed estimate equals the
A-only estimate to the last digit (e.g.\ st.308: 41\,177 vs.\ 8\,926), never an

returns $\approx 1.00$--$1.04$. Crucially, this is \emph{not} because the
optimizer ``picks the better statistic'': the planner walks its per-relation
list in OID order (== CREATE order) and uses the \emph{first} matching
statistic. Building the same pair with B first flips the winner --- st.144
(A$=$AFV, B$=$ACV) switches from A-only's 11\,894 (q 1.04) to B-only's 5\,827
(q 2.13) purely by swapping creation order. So co-installed overlap does not
``degrade'' per se; it makes the outcome \emph{depend on deployment order}, and
this is why disjoint deployments (Stage 3) restore exact predictability: with
no overlapping pairs the ``first-built'' choice is forced.

A systematic probe confirms interference is a \emph{step function of order}, not
of overlap amount: prepending a single bad sibling before a query's winner
lifts its E2E/predicted ratio from 1.0 to $\approx$1.8--4.1 (additional siblings
do not accumulate, since the planner uses the \emph{first} matching one), while
the naive overlap density $d$ stays $\approx$0.93--1.00 throughout --- so
overlap density alone cannot predict interference; only the deployment
\emph{order} of a bad sibling can.

\subsection{Stage 3: Global Disjointness Restores Near-Exact Predictability}
\label{sec:p1}

We fix the gap with the Option-A model: a \emph{global-disjointness} constraint

set to be pairwise column-disjoint, so the planner cannot cross-talk. Table
\ref{tab:p1} measures predicted vs E2E for both models at two budgets.

\subsubsection{Systematic validation: predictions across many deployments.}
\label{sec:e2esys}
The two deployments above are suggestive but few. We therefore run a
\emph{systematic} sweep: on the real \texttt{posts} table we deploy 5 singletons
plus the whole-workload budgeted MILP at four budgets (10/40/100/250\,KB),
each solved both \emph{with} global disjointness and \emph{without}, and measure
E2E for every query a deployment touches. Fig.~\ref{fig:e2epredict} summarizes

what a per-candidate measurement model predicts: individual statistics are
faithful, and the residual error is a joint effect of co-installed overlapping
statistics that disjointness removes --- and removes \emph{generally}, not just
in the original two deployments.

\subsection{The Causal Takeaway}
\label{sec:e2e-takeaway}

The three stages form a clean causal chain. (i) Per-candidate measurement is
sound, and a single deployed statistic is exactly predictable (Stage 1);
(ii) the only place prediction diverges is under co-installed \emph{overlapping}
statistics, where the planner's joint choice violates the per-candidate
independence assumption (Stage 2); and (iii) enforcing pairwise column
disjointness removes that interaction and restores ratio 1.000 (Stage 3), at a
quantified quality cost. This does not undermine the method --- it precisely
delimits where the sparse model holds, and provides a validated, deployable fix.
It is the empirical ``surprise'' that turns validation into a contribution; the
practical alternative when the quality cost of disjointness is unacceptable is
Option B: solve without the constraint, run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check,
drop any interfering statistic, and re-solve.

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Why Does Sparsity Occur? A Mechanistic Hypothesis}
\label{sec:disc-sparsity}

We stress that the sparse finding (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) has a mechanistic account as a
\emph{hypothesis}, not a theorem. A single-table selection query's error is
typically dominated by \emph{one} correlated column cluster: its restriction
clause is a conjunction of predicates whose selectivity error is largest where
columns are most strongly correlated (where independence fails hardest), and the
planner's default estimate is about as wrong as the worst such sub-clause. A
single MCV over exactly that dominant cluster removes the largest error source;
residual errors in weakly-correlated predicates contribute little. Hence
\emph{one} well-chosen statistic captures nearly all improvement attainable from
the 2-/3-column MCV candidates, and a second non-overlapping statistic finds no
independent ``second cluster'' worth capturing.

This is consistent with every observation we report: $k{=}2,3$ add nothing
(RQ2); the top-1 coverage CDF is sharply peaked near 1.0; capacity
\emph{upgrading} the dominant statistic matters more than adding others (RQ3);
and the sparse cap costs no quality in the ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}). It
also predicts the boundary of our claim. Workloads where a query is genuinely
driven by \emph{several} independent correlated clusters violate sparsity and
warrant the multi-select model ($\S\ref{sec:formulation}$); our controlled
stress test (\S\ref{sec:rq2}, Table \ref{tab:sweep}) confirms this directly --
a query over two independent clusters goes from top-1 $=$ 100 to top-2 $=$ 1.00,
and sparsity collapses once $K\ge 3$. Characterising how often that regime
occurs on real workloads is an open question below.

\subsection{What Is Being Optimized}
\label{sec:disc-objective}

We optimize a \emph{storage} budget (persistent bytes and estimation quality,
\S\ref{sec:rq3}); ANALYZE maintenance cost is handled separately because
PostgreSQL couples its sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} target on a
relation, so we treat capacity as an allocation of persistent storage. The
budget-quality curves in \S\ref{sec:rq3} and Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality} show
that a storage budget is, in practice, spent on the ``how much'' axis.

\emph{Why arithmetic mean q-error.} q-error is bounded below by 1 with a heavy
upper tail, so the arithmetic mean rewards repairing the worst queries --
exactly the cost of a bad estimate -- while the median stays 1.000. It also
keeps the MILP linear in the $\Delta_{is}$ terms (a geometric mean,
$\min\sum_i\log e_i$, would stay linear too while weighting \emph{relative}
improvement). We check the mean choice empirically: solving the sparse MILP
under the log objective and the linear objective picks overlapping-but-identical
statistic sets (Jaccard $0.67$--$0.83$) yet yields \emph{identical} resulting
arithmetic and geometric mean q-errors at every budget
(Table \ref{tab:meanrob}). The deployment is robust to the mean choice, and we
keep the arithmetic mean because it directly targets the worst queries that our
evaluation emphasizes.

\subsection{The Interference Fix}
\label{sec:disc-fix}

The validated fix (Option A, global disjointness) is the trustworthy main model:
it restores empirically near-exact predictability at a quantified quality cost
(\S\ref{sec:p1}).
As a practical fallback (Option B), one can solve without the constraint and
then run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check, drop any interfering statistic, and
re-solve. We recommend A as the default because it makes the deployment semantics
match the measurement model (\S\ref{sec:measure-correct}) --- the deployment is
then structurally independent, not just measured independently.

\subsection{Open Questions}

(1) \emph{Level monotonicity}: enforcing that a higher capacity never yields a
worse q-error could prune options and speed the solve. (2) \emph{Maintenance-aware
selection}: jointly modeling ANALYZE cost (a function of the \emph{max} target)
replaces the storage-only budget with an end-to-end maintenance budget.
(3) \emph{Upgrade vs.\ add}: our data show a smooth capacity-only curve on
stats\_CEB\_single and a large tail on CENSUS, but a principled
``upgrade-vs-add'' decision boundary --- e.g.\ a marginal-value heuristic
(quality gained per storage unit) --- is open. (4) \emph{When does sparsity
fail?}: identifying workloads with genuinely multiple independent correlated
clusters (the regime needing the multi-select model) is the most important open
question. (5) \emph{Limits}: we consider MCV statistics on single-table
workloads; joins need other techniques, there is a real limit on statistics per
relation, and our 2-/3-column candidate family understates achievable quality on
harder CENSUS queries, where 4-/5-column candidates help --- extending candidate
generation to higher arity is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Extended-statistics selection should be treated as a joint \emph{which} and
\emph{how much} decision. On the two single-table workloads we evaluate
(stats\_CEB\_single and the wide CENSUS benchmark) we observe a sparse regime
among the evaluated 2-/3-column MCV candidates --- one dominant statistic per
query --- which, together with capacity levels, makes the budget-constrained
problem an exactly-linear MILP solvable in seconds at full-workload scale, via
a catalog-mask protocol that makes per-candidate, per-capacity measurement
feasible on wide tables. End-to-end validation shows per-candidate prediction is
sound and isolates the residual risk to planner cross-talk among co-installed
overlapping statistics; we explain it as a creation-order effect
(\S\ref{sec:switch}) and restore empirically near-exact predictability either
with a conservative global-disjointness mode or with post-selection
planner-validation. Join-heavy workloads are the negative control.

\emph{Scope.} We state precisely what we do and do not claim. Our contribution
is not an MILP \emph{per se}, nor a claim that extended-statistics selection is
inherently sparse. It is the combination of three scoped findings: (i) a
\emph{capacity-aware} reformulation --- the ``what $\times$ how much'' budget
allocation across \texttt{statistics\_target} --- which is new and independent
of sparsity; (ii) a \emph{measurable sparse regime}, characterized empirically
on the two evaluated single-table workloads (with an explicit, synthetic
demonstration of where it fails); and (iii) a \emph{scalable measurement}
protocol that turns the candidate $\times$ capacity space into data the
optimizer can consume. Taken together --- not any single one --- these make
capacity-aware extended-statistics selection \emph{optimizable and
deployable} on the workloads we evaluate; the general (Boolean-multi-select,
arbitrary-statistic-kind, join-dominated) version of the problem is explicitly
out of scope.
