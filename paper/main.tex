\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern query optimizers rely on cardinality estimates to choose plans, and when
columns are correlated the default independence assumption can misestimate by
orders of magnitude. PostgreSQL exposes \emph{extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) to capture multivariate correlation, yet deciding
\emph{which} statistics to create --- and, crucially, \emph{how precisely} to
store them --- is left to the database administrator. This paper asks whether
that choice can be automated, and what structure it has in practice.

A statistic is not atomic. Each has a \emph{capacity}, the number of MCV
entries it stores, controlled by its \texttt{statistics\_target} (100, 1000, or
10000). Capacity determines both storage and estimation precision: a
3-column correlation that is invisible at target 100 becomes accurate at 1000
and exact at 10000. Extended-statistics selection is therefore a joint
decision over two dimensions:
\[
  \underbrace{\text{which }(\text{table},\text{columns})}_{\text{what}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{which }\texttt{statistics\_target}}_{\text{how much}},
\]
compounded by a combinatorial explosion in candidates --- a single CENSUS query
can have up to 455 candidate column combinations (19{,}245 deduplicated across
the workload). We are not aware of prior work that optimizes this joint
``selection $\times$ capacity'' problem under a storage budget.

Three empirical observations, all verified end-to-end on PostgreSQL 16, shape
our approach. \textbf{First, capacity materially affects repair quality.}
An ANALYZE at target 100 loses 3-column correlation, while 1000 captures it and
10000 is exact; under a budget, the right move is often to \emph{degrade
capacity} (store a statistic at a lower target) rather than to drop it --- a
\emph{graceful degradation} that a pure ``which columns'' view cannot express.
\textbf{Second, the benefit is strikingly sparse.} Across single-table
workloads (stats\_CEB and the wide 69-column CENSUS), one well-chosen
multivariate statistic suffices per query: building a second or third
non-overlapping candidate adds almost nothing. This makes the objective
\emph{exactly linear} (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}) and the resulting budgeted
mixed-integer program solvable in seconds at full-workload scale. \textbf{Third,
co-installed overlapping statistics interfere.} When several overlapping MCVs
are active on one table, the planner can pick a sub-optimal one; end-to-end
measurement shows predictions are individually accurate but jointly violated by
this interaction, which a global-disjointness constraint removes.

We make the following contributions. (i) We reformulate extended-statistics
selection as \emph{capacity-aware selection} --- a budgeted MILP over
$(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ objects (\S\ref{sec:formulation},
\S\ref{sec:milp}). (ii) We report the sparse empirical structure that makes it
tractable: one dominant statistic per query (\S\ref{sec:rq2}), with a
formal linearity proposition. (iii) We contribute a catalog-mask measurement
protocol (\S\ref{sec:measure}) that makes per-candidate, per-capacity estimation
feasible on wide tables at $\sim$60$\times$ lower cost. (iv) We validate
end-to-end: predictions are sound, and expose the planner-interference effect
with a fix that restores exact predictability (\S\ref{sec:p1}). The approach
scales to the full CENSUS workload --- 57{,}735 physical statistics, 150{,}303
binary variables --- solved optimally in under five seconds
(\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:back}

\subsection{Cardinality Estimation and Multi-column Correlation}

Query optimizers plan around estimated cardinalities. PostgreSQL's default
estimator uses per-column histograms and most-common-value (MCV) lists and
assumes columns are independent; when columns are correlated this assumption
over- or under-counts by orders of magnitude. \emph{Extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) address this by capturing multivariate structure,
in three kinds --- MCV lists, \texttt{ndistinct}, and functional
\emph{dependencies}. Empirically the MCV kind is the most effective for
selection selectivity, so in this paper we focus on MCV statistics.

\subsection{How PostgreSQL Actually Uses Extended Statistics}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Several mechanism facts (verified from the PostgreSQL 16 source) ground our
model. \textbf{Scope.} Extended statistics are consulted only for
\emph{restriction} (WHERE) clauses on a \emph{single relation}:
the selectivity entry point is gated by a single-relation check, and
\texttt{eqjoinsel} never consults \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext}. Consequently
\textbf{extended statistics cannot improve JOIN estimation} --- their value is
confined to single-table selection predicates. This is why our evaluation
separates single-table workloads (where they act) from join workloads (a
negative control).

\textbf{Capacity is per-object and tunable.} Each statistic can be assigned its
own \texttt{statistics\_target} via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}
(stored in \texttt{stxstattarget}). But \textbf{ANALYZE samples a number of rows
equal to the maximum \texttt{statistics\_target} over the relation's
statistics}, so one high-target statistic drags the whole ANALYZE to a larger
sample (a full scan at 10000). This couples capacity to \emph{maintenance cost},
not only storage. Because of this coupling we deliberately optimise a
\emph{storage} budget --- the persistent bytes of the selected statistics and the
estimation quality they deliver --- and treat ANALYZE maintenance cost
separately (it is a property of the maximum, not the sum, of targets).

\textbf{Capacity-quality trade-off.} Measured on the CENSUS \texttt{climate}
table (69 columns), the repair quality of the best single statistic is roughly
2.1--2.3 at target 100, 1.2--2.1 at 1000, and $\approx$1.0 at 10000. Target 100
loses 3-column correlation entirely; target 1000 captures it at a fraction of
the ANALYZE cost of 10000.

\subsection{Related Work}

Four bodies of work are relevant. (a) \emph{Cardinality estimation}: histogram
and MCV-based selectivity, the independence assumption, and q-error as a
quality metric; our problem is not estimate \emph{construction} but statistic
\emph{deployment}. (b) \emph{Automatic statistics} in commercial systems:
these choose \emph{which} column combinations to create, typically under a
maintenance budget, but to our knowledge do not jointly optimize the
per-statistic capacity (\texttt{statistics\_target}). The capacity dimension,
and the budget-as-capacity-reallocation view, is our distinguishing novelty.
(c) \emph{Physical-design selection} (index and materialized-view selection)
solves budget-constrained structure selection with MILP and greedy heuristics;
our MILP follows the same methodology but targets statistics with a capacity
axis, and exploits an empirically validated sparsity that linearizes the
objective. (d) \emph{Learned cardinality estimation} trains models to estimate
selectivity directly; unlike those, we drive the \emph{real} PostgreSQL planner
and measure its actual q-error, so no synthetic model accuracy is involved.
A more complete citation set is the subject of ongoing work (see
\texttt{references.bib}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Notation and Objective}
\label{sec:notn}

We are given a workload of queries $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. The accuracy of the
planner's estimate for $q_i$ is the \textbf{q-error}
\[
  \operatorname{qerr}(\text{est},\text{act})=\frac{\max(\text{est},\text{act})}
  {\min(\text{est},\text{act})}\ge 1.
\]
Let $e_i^0$ be the baseline q-error of $q_i$ with no extended statistics. A
\emph{physical statistic} is $s=(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{level})$,
with storage cost $c_s$ (bytes) and a per-query effective q-error $e_{is}$
when $q_i$ uses only $s$. The selection objective is
\[
  \min_{\{y_s\},\{x_{is}\}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{qerr}_i\big(T_i\big)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_s c_s\,y_s \le C,
\]
where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ says statistic $s$ is created and $T_i$ is the set of
created statistics used by $q_i$. Because a statistic can serve many queries,
we use the assignment $x_{is}\le y_s$ so each \emph{physical} object is built
once and its storage paid once in the budget --- the shared-resource semantics
that make global selection different from per-query greedy.

\subsection{Multi-select vs.\ the Sparse Special Case}
\label{sec:sparse}

In the general \emph{multi-select} case a query may use several statistics, and
the joint effect is not directly measurable for every subset. A standard
approach is the multiplicative approximation in log space,
\[
  \log e_i(T_i)\;\approx\;\log e_i^0 + \sum_{s\in T_i}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{e_{is}}{e_i^0}\Big),
\]
which is exact only when the chosen statistics act independently, and can
break when overlapping statistics coexist (we return to this in
\S\ref{sec:e2e}). Our experiments ($\S\ref{sec:rq2}$) reveal that per query a
\emph{single dominant statistic} suffices, so we specialise to the
\emph{sparse} model: each query selects at most one statistic,
$\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$. Under this restriction the objective is exactly linear.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linearity under per-query unit cap]
\label{prop:linear}
If each query selects at most one statistic ($\sum_s x_{is} \le 1$), then the
per-query q-error is exactly
\[
  e_i\big(T_i\big)\;=\;e_i^0\;-\;\sum_s \big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)\,x_{is},
\]
with no interaction term, so the workload objective is linear in the selection
variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a fixed query $i$, the selected set $T_i$ is either empty or a singleton
$\{s\}$. In the former case $e_i(T_i)=e_i^0$; in the latter,
$e_i(T_i)=e_{is}$. Since at most one $x_{is}$ equals $1$, no two statistics
co-occur and there is no joint term.
\end{proof}

The log-space formulation used for multi-select is therefore unnecessary here;
we solve the linear form directly. We call this the \emph{capacity-aware}
selection problem: because each capacity level is a distinct physical
statistic, choosing \emph{which} level of a combination to build is expressed
by selecting the corresponding $y_{(table,cols,level)}$, subject to a global
budget.

\section{Optimization: Sparse MILP with Capacity Levels}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsection{Variables and Objective}

We solve the sparse model as a mixed-integer linear program. Binary variables
are $y_s$ (create physical statistic $s$) and $x_{is}$ (query $i$ uses $s$).
By Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}, minimizing mean q-error is equivalent to
maximizing total improvement,
\[
  \max \sum_{i,s}\underbrace{\big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)}_{\Delta_{is}\ge 0}x_{is},
\]
where an option with $\Delta_{is}\le 0$ (no better than baseline) is pruned in
advance.

\subsection{Constraints}

The MILP enforces: (1) the storage budget $\sum_s c_s y_s\le C$; (2) a statistic
must exist to be used, $x_{is}\le y_s$ (shared across queries); (3) sparse
per-query cap $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$; and (4) capacity-level exclusivity
$\sum_{\ell} y_{(cols,\ell)}\le 1$ --- a real object has a single
\texttt{statistics\_target}, so at most one level of a given combination is
created. A fifth, optional constraint enforces \emph{global disjointness}:
$y_a+y_b\le 1$ whenever the column sets of $a$ and $b$ overlap. This is
stronger than (4) (which only forbids two levels of the \emph{same}
combination) and removes the residual planner cross-talk among co-installed
overlapping MCVs ($\S\ref{sec:p1}$).

\subsection{Pruning and Solve Scalability}
\label{sec:scale}

Two factors keep the MILP small. First, \texttt{skip\_worse\_than\_baseline}
drops any option whose per-query q-error is not better than the no-stat
baseline; because baseline estimates are usually already good, most options are
pruned, leaving only genuine ``repairers''. Second, global deduplication means
$n_{\text{stats}}$ is the deduplicated universe of
$(\text{table},\text{cols},\text{level})$ rather than the per-query sum. On the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the resulting problem is large but trivially
solvable: the worst-case (no pruning) instance has 57{,}735 physical
statistics, 92{,}568 options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved
optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280
statistics / 46{,}392 options / 80{,}672 variables ($\approx$4.3s). Thus the
optimization is not the bottleneck at full-workload scale --- the phase-1
\emph{measurement} is (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\section{Measurement Infrastructure}
\label{sec:measure}

Our ILP needs a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error $e_{is}$ and size $c_s$
for every option that survives pruning. The naive \emph{Protocol-A}
(CREATE $\to$ ANALYZE $\to$ EXPLAIN $\to$ DROP $\to$ ANALYZE for each candidate)
is infeasible on wide tables: a single CENSUS ANALYZE at target 10000 takes
$\sim$20s, and with 1000 candidate statistics the same ANALYZE takes
$\sim$264s.

We instead use the \textbf{catalog-mask protocol (Protocol-M)}. For each table,
all candidate statistics are created once and built by a \emph{single} ANALYZE
(sampling at the maximum target). To measure one candidate in isolation, we
\emph{mask} the others by NULL-ing their payload in
\texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data} --- the planner ignores statistics whose
payload is absent --- then EXPLAIN, and restore the payloads from a temporary
backup table via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM} (because \texttt{pg\_mcv\_list} has no
bytea cast for a driver round-trip). Multi-capacity measurement uses one object
per (candidate, level) with \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}, again
built by a single ANALYZE at the maximum target. Protocol-M reduces the cost of a
per-candidate, per-level measurement by roughly $60\times$, making the CENSUS
and full-workload studies feasible.

\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{RQ1: Does Capacity Materially Affect Repair Quality?}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Yes.} On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table, the best single statistic
brings a high-error query to a q-error of roughly 2.1--2.3 at target 100,
1.2--2.1 at 1000, and $\approx$1.0 at 10000 (Table \ref{tab:capacity}). Target
100 cannot even represent the relevant 3-column correlation; target 1000
captures it but leaves residual error, and 10000 is near-exact. Capacity is
therefore not a storage knob that can be swept under the rug --- it sets the
estimation precision directly. This motivates treating
$(\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ as a joint decision (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}).

\subsection{RQ2: Is One Statistic per Query Sufficient?}
\label{sec:rq2}

\textbf{Surprisingly, yes.} For each query we greedily rank non-overlapping
candidates by single q-error and measure the \emph{joint} q-error of keeping the
top-1/2/3. On stats\_CEB\_single the joint q-error at $k=2,3$ equals $k=1$
within 0.02--0.03; on the wide CENSUS table, \texttt{query.184} (4162 baseline,
165 candidates) and \texttt{query.382} (378, 56 candidates) are identical at
$k{=}1,2,3$ at both targets (Table \ref{tab:multi}). This is not an artefact of
a small candidate space: it holds on the widest, highest-error,
most-candidate-dense workload in our study. One dominant column cluster drives a
single-table query's error, and one well-chosen 3-column MCV captures it.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the MILP Beat a Budgeted Greedy?}
\label{sec:rq3}

We are careful here. With a \emph{generous} budget, the per-query-best greedy
union already equals the ILP solution (same 5 statistics, mean 1.021) --- on a
workload of independent single-table queries, greedy is already near-optimal.
The ILP's distinctive value appears under \emph{tight} budgets, where it
reallocates \emph{capacity} rather than dropping coverage. On the full 632-query
stats\_CEB\_single workload (Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}), the budget is
visibly spent on capacity: the number of high-capacity selections grows with
budget (L10000: $1\to\cdots\to15$, L1000: $1\to\cdots\to15$), and the clearest
case, \texttt{posts(AnswerCount,ViewCount)}, upgrades from L100 to L1000 to
L10000 as the budget grows --- the same \emph{what}, more \emph{how much}. The
multi-metric view confirms that mean is outlier-dominated (median stays 1.000);
the real effect is \emph{tail repair}, with the maximum q-error falling from
4.7 to 1.1. On the huge-tail CENSUS top-10 the improvement is across the whole
distribution: baseline (mean / median / P90 / max) $=$
(1071.5 / 332.5 / 2542.9 / 4162.1) collapses to $(1.10 / 1.09 / 1.18 / 1.41)$.
We optimise mean but evaluate the full distribution; the headline is the tail
collapse, e.g.\ a 4162 $\to$ 1.4 maximum.

\subsection{RQ4: Does the Approach Scale?}
\label{sec:rq4}

\emph{The optimization is not the bottleneck.} On the full 468-query CENSUS
workload the worst-case (no-prune) MILP has 57{,}735 physical statistics, 92{,}568
options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved optimally by HiGHS in
$\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280 / 46{,}392 / 80{,}672 and
solves in $\approx$4.3s. Measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) dominates the
pipeline, not the solve.

\subsection{RQ5: Does Prediction Survive Real PostgreSQL Execution?}
\label{sec:rq5}

We split this into two parts (detailed in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\emph{(a) Are individual candidate measurements predictive?} Yes --- building a
single selected statistic alone reproduces its mask-measured q-error exactly.
\emph{(b) Are co-installed overlapping statistics independent?} No --- when
several overlapping MCVs coexist, the planner can switch a query to a
sub-optimal overlapping statistic. This second-order interaction is the one
place where the sparse model's prediction and the real system diverge, and it is
removed by the global-disjointness constraint (\S\ref{sec:p1}).

\subsection{RQ6: Does It Help Where Extended Statistics Cannot?}
\label{sec:rq6}

\textbf{No meaningful benefit --- as a sanity check.} On the join-heavy
\texttt{JOB} workload only 37/113 queries even have a multi-column selection
candidate and, after deduplication, just 7 distinct combinations exist; because
its errors are dominated by joins (which extended statistics cannot touch,
\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}), no amount of capacity tuning helps. This is the
expected null result for the negative control: the method does not optimise for
coverage's sake.

\section{End-to-End Validation}
\label{sec:e2e}

\subsection{Experiment}

We close the loop by building the ILP-selected set on the real database and
comparing predicted to measured q-error. For the 2 MB solution on
stats\_CEB\_single (5 statistics, predicted mean 1.021) we create each object
with its per-object \texttt{statistics\_target} and ANALYZE once; the measured
mean is \textbf{1.32} --- a 1.29$\times$ gap. Five of the eight queries match
their prediction almost exactly (e.g.\ st.562: 1.003, st.308: 1.000), while
three (st.144, st.588, st.284) degrade from $\approx$1.0 to 2.13 / 2.07 / 1.90.

\subsection{Attribution: Concurrent Overlapping Stats Interfere}

The three degraded queries are all served by the same statistic,
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)}. Building \emph{only} that
statistic gives 1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 --- matching the mask prediction --- but
with all five co-installed (all pairwise-overlapping MCVs over
\{AnswerCount, FavoriteCount, ViewCount, PostTypeId\}) the planner switches
those queries to a different overlapping statistic. This precisely isolates the
cause: \textbf{per-candidate measurement is sound} (the singleton control is
the predicted value), and the residual error at deployment is a second-order
planner interaction \emph{among co-installed overlapping statistics}, exactly
the independence assumption that the sparse model relies on. Note this is not a
failure to predict a single statistic; it is a joint effect the per-candidate
model cannot see.

predicted vs E2E for both models at two budgets (Table \ref{tab:p1}).

The global-disjoint solution is \emph{exactly} predictable (ratio 1.000 at both
budgets), because the deployed set is pairwise column-disjoint and the planner
cannot cross-talk --- the independence assumption holds structurally rather than
per-query. The cost is a well-defined nominal-quality loss (predicted 2.00 vs
1.02): disjointness forces the solver to forgo overlapping-but-high-value
dominant candidates. This sharply isolates the two facts: (i) per-candidate
measurement is sound (disjoint ratios are 1.000); (ii) the overlap model's
residual error is entirely planner interference among co-installed overlapping
statistics (ratio 1.18--1.29). Option A therefore yields a \emph{trustworthy}
deployment at a quantified quality cost.

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:disc}

Three observations frame the broader picture. \textbf{Why one dominant
candidate.} A single correlated column cluster drives single-table selection
error, so one well-chosen MCV captures nearly all attainable improvement; this
is the source of the sparsity that linearizes the problem. \textbf{What is being
optimised.} We optimise a \emph{storage} budget (persistent bytes and estimation
quality); ANALYZE maintenance cost is handled separately because PostgreSQL
couples its sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} target on a relation. This
keeps the objective clean. \textbf{The interference fix.} The validated fix
(Option A, global disjointness) is the trustworthy main model; as a practical
fallback (Option B) one can solve without the constraint and then run a
lightweight EXPLAIN-based check, dropping any interfering statistic and
re-solving.

Open questions remain. (1) \emph{Level monotonicity}: enforcing that a higher
capacity never yields a worse q-error could prune options and speed the solve,
but is not required for correctness. (2) \emph{Maintenance-aware selection}:
jointly modelling ANALYZE cost (as a function of the max target) rather than
storage alone is a natural extension. (3) \emph{Where the budget is best spent}:
our data show a smooth capacity-only curve for stats\_CEB\_single and a large
tail on CENSUS, but a principled characterisation of ``when to upgrade capacity
vs add coverage'' is open. (4) \emph{Limits}: we consider MCV statistics on
single-table workloads; join workloads need other techniques, and the real
system has a limit on the number of statistics per relation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Extended-statistics selection should be treated as a joint \emph{which} and
\emph{how much} decision. Across single-table workloads we find the benefit is
strikingly sparse---one dominant statistic per query---which makes the
budget-constrained problem an exactly-linear MILP solvable in seconds at
full-workload scale, through a catalog-mask protocol that makes per-candidate,
per-capacity measurement feasible on wide tables. End-to-end validation shows
per-candidate prediction is sound and isolates the residual risk to planner
cross-talk among co-installed overlapping statistics; a global-disjointness
constraint restores exact predictability. Join-heavy workloads are the negative
control. The result is a principled, scalable, and honestly-validated recipe
for automating PostgreSQL extended-statistics deployment under a storage
budget.
