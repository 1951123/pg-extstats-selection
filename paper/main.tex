\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern query optimizers rely on cardinality estimates to choose plans, and when
columns are correlated the default independence assumption can misestimate by
orders of magnitude. PostgreSQL exposes \emph{extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) to capture multivariate correlation, yet deciding
\emph{which} statistics to create --- and, crucially, \emph{how precisely} to
store them --- is left to the database administrator. This paper asks whether
that choice can be automated, and what structure it has in practice.

\textbf{(A) The problem: a joint ``what and how much'' allocation.} A statistic
is not atomic. Each has a \emph{capacity}, the number of MCV entries it stores,
controlled by its \texttt{statistics\_target} (100, 1000, or 10000). Capacity
determines both storage and estimation precision: a 3-column correlation that is
invisible at target 100 becomes accurate at 1000 and exact at 10000. Automated
statistics management is a well-studied problem (Chaudhuri and Narasayya
\cite{chaudhuri2000automating}), but it chooses \emph{which} statistics to
create. Extended-statistics selection is richer: a storage budget can be spent
either by \emph{adding} a statistic or by \emph{upgrading an existing one}
from L100 to L1000 to L10000. The right question is therefore a
\emph{resource-allocation} one --- given a budget, which
$(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ objects to instantiate --- not
merely which column combinations:
\[
  \underbrace{\text{which }(\text{table},\text{columns})}_{\text{what}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\text{which }\texttt{statistics\_target}}_{\text{how much}},
\]
compounded by a combinatorial explosion in candidates --- a single CENSUS query
can have up to 455 candidate column combinations (19{,}245 deduplicated across
the workload). To our knowledge, no prior work optimizes this joint
selection-and-capacity problem under a storage budget.

\emph{Running example.} Consider a query filtering
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount, ViewCount)}. A statistic over this combination can
be stored at L100, L1000, or L10000. Measured on our workload, L100 is too
coarse to capture the correlation (q-error stays $\sim$4.7), while L10000
drives it to $\approx$1.0. So the answer to ``should we build this statistic?'' is
incomplete without the second question: ``\emph{at what capacity}?'' --- and
since L10000 costs $\sim$100KB while L100 costs $\sim$2KB, the choice under a
budget is genuinely a \emph{how much} decision. This is the ``what and how much''
allocation we optimize: the same combination can be deployed at DIFFERENT
capacities depending on the budget, and spending on \emph{upgrading} it competes
directly with spending on \emph{adding} another combination.

\textbf{(B) The surprise: the benefit is almost entirely sparse.} At first
glance the problem looks like a combinatorial multi-select: a query could
benefit from several statistics at once. Our measurements show otherwise.
Across both single-table workloads (the wide 69-column CENSUS and
stats\_CEB\_single), one well-chosen multivariate statistic captures nearly all
attainable improvement for a query: adding a second or third non-overlapping
candidate gives almost no benefit, and on the hardest CENSUS queries the top-1,
top-2 and top-3 joint q-errors are identical. This was not assumed --- it was
\textbf{discovered} --- and it has a direct consequence: restricted to at most
one statistic per query, the objective has no interaction term and is
\emph{exactly linear} (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}), turning a hard nonlinear
selection problem into a budgeted mixed-integer program.

\textbf{(C) The enabler: measuring the massive candidate space.} Instantiating
this optimization requires a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error for every
surviving option. On wide tables the naive protocol (CREATE--ANALYZE--EXPLAIN
per candidate) is infeasible --- a single CENSUS ANALYZE at target 10000 takes
$\sim$20s. Our \emph{catalog-mask protocol} (\S\ref{sec:measure}) builds all
candidates in one ANALYZE and isolates each by masking the others'
\texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data} payloads, cutting measurement cost by
$\sim$60$\times$ and making the enormous candidate $\times$ capacity space
actually measurable. The resulting MILP is then the easy part: the full CENSUS
workload --- 57{,}735 physical statistics, 150{,}303 binary variables --- solves
optimally in under five seconds (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\textbf{(D) The validation surprise: overlapping statistics interfere.}
End-to-end deployment on real PostgreSQL shows per-candidate predictions are
accurate, but co-installed \emph{overlapping} statistics let the planner switch
queries to a sub-optimal statistic (predicted mean 1.02 vs.\ measured 1.32). A
\emph{global-disjointness} constraint restores exact predictability
(ratio 1.000) at a quantified quality cost (\S\ref{sec:p1}). This is not a
failure of our measurement --- it is a second-order planner interaction that the
per-candidate model cannot see, and a concrete, validated fix.

In summary, we contribute (i) a reformulation of extended-statistics selection
as budgeted \emph{what-and-how-much allocation} (\S\ref{sec:formulation},
\S\ref{sec:milp}); (ii) an empirical sparse-structure finding with a formal
linearity proposition that makes the problem tractable (\S\ref{sec:rq2}); (iii)
the catalog-mask measurement protocol that makes the candidate space measurable
(\S\ref{sec:measure}); and (iv) an end-to-end validation that isolates --- and
fixes --- the planner-interference effect (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:back}

\subsection{Cardinality Estimation and Multi-column Correlation}

Query optimizers plan around estimated cardinalities. PostgreSQL's default
estimator uses per-column histograms and most-common-value (MCV) lists and
assumes columns are independent; when columns are correlated this assumption
over- or under-counts by orders of magnitude. \emph{Extended statistics}
(\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS}) address this by capturing multivariate structure,
in three kinds --- MCV lists, \texttt{ndistinct}, and functional
\emph{dependencies}. Empirically the MCV kind is the most effective for
selection selectivity, so in this paper we focus on MCV statistics.

\subsection{How PostgreSQL Actually Uses Extended Statistics}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

Several mechanism facts (verified from the PostgreSQL 16 source) ground our
model. \textbf{Scope.} Extended statistics are consulted only for
\emph{restriction} (WHERE) clauses on a \emph{single relation}:
the selectivity entry point is gated by a single-relation check, and
\texttt{eqjoinsel} never consults \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext}. Consequently
\textbf{extended statistics cannot improve JOIN estimation} --- their value is
confined to single-table selection predicates. This is why our evaluation
separates single-table workloads (where they act) from join workloads (a
negative control).

\textbf{Capacity is per-object and tunable.} Each statistic can be assigned its
own \texttt{statistics\_target} via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}
(stored in \texttt{stxstattarget}). But \textbf{ANALYZE samples a number of rows
equal to the maximum \texttt{statistics\_target} over the relation's
statistics}, so one high-target statistic drags the whole ANALYZE to a larger
sample (a full scan at 10000). This couples capacity to \emph{maintenance cost},
not only storage. Because of this coupling we deliberately optimise a
\emph{storage} budget --- the persistent bytes of the selected statistics and the
estimation quality they deliver --- and treat ANALYZE maintenance cost
separately (it is a property of the maximum, not the sum, of targets).

\textbf{Capacity-quality trade-off.} Measured on the CENSUS \texttt{climate}
table (69 columns), the repair quality of the best single statistic is roughly
2.1--2.3 at target 100, 1.2--2.1 at 1000, and $\approx$1.0 at 10000. Target 100
loses 3-column correlation entirely; target 1000 captures it at a fraction of
the ANALYZE cost of 10000.

\subsection{Related Work}

We position our work against five bodies of literature; for each we say what it
\emph{optimizes}, \emph{measures}, and why it does not solve the
\emph{what} $\times$ \emph{how much} question.

\textbf{(a) Cardinality estimation.} Histograms and MCVs \cite{ioannidis2003cardinality},
selectivity from query feedback \cite{chendependencies}, and -- more recently --
\emph{learned} cardinality models \cite{kipf2019learned} all aim to improve the
estimate itself. Our problem is orthogonal: we do not estimate, we decide
\emph{which statistics to deploy} so that PostgreSQL's own estimator is
accurate. In particular, we drive the \emph{real} planner and measure its actual
q-error, so no synthetic model accuracy is involved.

\textbf{(b) Automatic statistics management.} Chaudhuri and Narasayya
\cite{chaudhuri2000automating} showed that a workload's needed statistics can be
identified and pruned to a small effective set. This establishes the
\emph{which} dimension as a solved (or at least heavily-studied) problem --- and
is exactly why we do \emph{not} claim novelty for ``automatically selecting
statistics.'' The gap we address is the second axis: prior work chooses column
combinations under a cost but does not jointly choose each statistic's
\emph{capacity} (\texttt{statistics\_target}). Concretely, prior work decides
\emph{whether to add} a statistic; we additionally decide \emph{whether to
upgrade} an already-chosen one from L100 to L1000 to L10000. That is the shift
from ``selection'' to ``budget allocation.''

\textbf{(c) Physical database design and index selection.} Budget-constrained
structure selection is a classic problem: which indexes to build, typically
solved by cost-driven candidate generation plus MILP or greedy
\cite{chaudhuri1997index,indexselection}. Our MILP follows the same methodology.
The novelty is twofold: our candidates carry a \emph{capacity} axis (an index
has no analogue of $\texttt{statistics\_target}$), and our objective is
\emph{provably linear} because we exploit a \emph{discovered sparse structure}
($\S\ref{sec:rq2}$) --- a property index-selection workloads do not exhibit. The
result is that the hard part of the problem is not the optimization (seconds at
full scale) but the \emph{measurement} of the candidate space
(\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\textbf{(d) PostgreSQL extended statistics.} The mechanics of
\texttt{CREATE STATISTICS} --- MCV / ndistinct / dependencies, per-object
\texttt{statistics\_target}, the restriction to single relations, and the
ANALYZE sampling coupling \cite{pgstats,pgmv} --- are the substrate of our
model (\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). To our knowledge no prior work formulates
extended-statistics \emph{deployment} as a budgeted joint selection-and-capacity
problem, nor provides a catalog-mask protocol to measure the candidate space at
scale.

\textbf{(e) Workload-driven database tuning.} Automated physical tuning
\cite{indexselection}, knob/configuration optimization, and workload-aware
index advisors share our high-level framing (workload $\to$ candidates $\to$
optimizer evaluation $\to$ global selection). What distinguishes our accuracy
criterion is that we optimise \emph{prediction quality under a storage budget}
(mean q-error), not wall-clock latency or a cost-benchmark proxy, and that we
validate the resulting deployment end-to-end against the real optimizer ---
surfacing a planner-interaction effect no tuning system that assumes independent
statistics would hit (\S\ref{sec:e2e}).

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Notation and Objective}
\label{sec:notn}

We are given a workload of queries $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$. The accuracy of the
planner's estimate for $q_i$ is the \textbf{q-error}
\[
  \operatorname{qerr}(\text{est},\text{act})=\frac{\max(\text{est},\text{act})}
  {\min(\text{est},\text{act})}\ge 1.
\]
Let $e_i^0$ be the baseline q-error of $q_i$ with no extended statistics. A
\emph{physical statistic} is $s=(\text{table},\text{columns},\text{level})$,
with storage cost $c_s$ (bytes) and a per-query effective q-error $e_{is}$
when $q_i$ uses only $s$. The selection objective is
\[
  \min_{\{y_s\},\{x_{is}\}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{qerr}_i\big(T_i\big)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_s c_s\,y_s \le C,
\]
where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ says statistic $s$ is created and $T_i$ is the set of
created statistics used by $q_i$. Because a statistic can serve many queries,
we use the assignment $x_{is}\le y_s$ so each \emph{physical} object is built
once and its storage paid once in the budget --- the shared-resource semantics
that make global selection different from per-query greedy.

\subsection{Multi-select vs.\ the Sparse Special Case}
\label{sec:sparse}

In the general \emph{multi-select} case a query may use several statistics, and
the joint effect is not directly measurable for every subset. A standard
approach is the multiplicative approximation in log space,
\[
  \log e_i(T_i)\;\approx\;\log e_i^0 + \sum_{s\in T_i}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{e_{is}}{e_i^0}\Big),
\]
which is exact only when the chosen statistics act independently, and can
break when overlapping statistics coexist (we return to this in
\S\ref{sec:e2e}). Our measurements (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) reveal --- surprisingly ---
that per query a \emph{single dominant statistic} already captures nearly all
attainable improvement, so we specialise to the \emph{sparse} model: each query
selects at most one statistic, $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$. This restriction is not a
modelling convenience but an observed property of the workloads, and under it
the objective is exactly linear.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linearity under per-query unit cap]
\label{prop:linear}
If each query selects at most one statistic ($\sum_s x_{is} \le 1$), then the
per-query q-error is exactly
\[
  e_i\big(T_i\big)\;=\;e_i^0\;-\;\sum_s \big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)\,x_{is},
\]
with no interaction term, so the workload objective is linear in the selection
variables.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a fixed query $i$, the selected set $T_i$ is either empty or a singleton
$\{s\}$. In the former case $e_i(T_i)=e_i^0$; in the latter,
$e_i(T_i)=e_{is}$. Since at most one $x_{is}$ equals $1$, no two statistics
co-occur and there is no joint term.
\end{proof}

The log-space formulation used for multi-select is therefore unnecessary here;
we solve the linear form directly. We call this the \emph{capacity-aware}
selection problem: because each capacity level is a distinct physical
statistic, choosing \emph{which} level of a combination to build is expressed
by selecting the corresponding $y_{(table,cols,level)}$, subject to a global
budget.

\section{Optimization: Sparse MILP with Capacity Levels}
\label{sec:milp}

\subsection{Variables and Objective}

We solve the sparse model as a mixed-integer linear program. Binary variables
are $y_s$ (create physical statistic $s$) and $x_{is}$ (query $i$ uses $s$).
By Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}, minimizing mean q-error is equivalent to
maximizing total improvement,
\[
  \max \sum_{i,s}\underbrace{\big(e_i^0-e_{is}\big)}_{\Delta_{is}\ge 0}x_{is},
\]
where an option with $\Delta_{is}\le 0$ (no better than baseline) is pruned in
advance.

\subsection{Constraints}

The MILP enforces: (1) the storage budget $\sum_s c_s y_s\le C$; (2) a statistic
must exist to be used, $x_{is}\le y_s$ (shared across queries); (3) sparse
per-query cap $\sum_s x_{is}\le 1$; and (4) capacity-level exclusivity
$\sum_{\ell} y_{(cols,\ell)}\le 1$ --- a real object has a single
\texttt{statistics\_target}, so at most one level of a given combination is
created. A fifth, optional constraint enforces \emph{global disjointness}:
$y_a+y_b\le 1$ whenever the column sets of $a$ and $b$ overlap. This is
stronger than (4) (which only forbids two levels of the \emph{same}
combination) and removes the residual planner cross-talk among co-installed
overlapping MCVs ($\S\ref{sec:p1}$).

\subsection{Pruning and Solve Scalability}
\label{sec:scale}

Two factors keep the MILP small. First, \texttt{skip\_worse\_than\_baseline}
drops any option whose per-query q-error is not better than the no-stat
baseline; because baseline estimates are usually already good, most options are
pruned, leaving only genuine ``repairers''. Second, global deduplication means
$n_{\text{stats}}$ is the deduplicated universe of
$(\text{table},\text{cols},\text{level})$ rather than the per-query sum. On the
full 468-query CENSUS workload the resulting problem is large but trivially
solvable: the worst-case (no pruning) instance has 57{,}735 physical
statistics, 92{,}568 options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved
optimally by HiGHS in $\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280
statistics / 46{,}392 options / 80{,}672 variables ($\approx$4.3s). Thus the
optimization is not the bottleneck at full-workload scale --- the phase-1
\emph{measurement} is (\S\ref{sec:measure}).

\section{Measurement Infrastructure}
\label{sec:measure}

Our ILP needs a per-candidate, per-capacity q-error $e_{is}$ and size $c_s$
for every option that survives pruning. This section turns the measurement
from an afterthought into a first-class systems component: it is, in fact, the
pipeline's true bottleneck (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\subsection{Naive Measurement Is Infeasible on Wide Tables}
\label{sec:measure-naive}

The obvious protocol (\emph{Protocol-A}) isolates a candidate by
\[
  \text{CREATE} \to \text{ANALYZE} \to \text{EXPLAIN} \to \text{DROP} \to \text{ANALYZE},
\]
a clean, drop-analyzable cycle that costs two ANALYZEs per candidate. On a wide
table this is prohibitive. On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table (69 columns,
$\sim$2.5M rows) a single ANALYZE at \texttt{statistics\_target}=10000 takes
$\sim$21.9s with no statistics, and grows with the number of statistics already
on the table (Table \ref{tab:measurement}). Consequently the naive protocol
costs $\sim$44s per candidate --- 44 seconds $\times$ 19{,}245 deduplicated
candidates is not a tractable phase-1.

\subsection{The Catalog-Mask Protocol (Protocol-M)}
\label{sec:measure-mask}

Our \textbf{catalog-mask protocol} builds all of a table's candidate statistics
\emph{once}, in a single ANALYZE (sampled at the maximum target), and measures
each candidate in isolation by \emph{masking} the others. Masking NULLs the
other candidates' payload in \texttt{pg\_statistic\_ext\_data}; the planner's
\texttt{statext\_is\_kind\_built()} test then omits a NULL-payload statistic from
\texttt{rel->statlist}, so the planner behaves as if that statistic did not
exist --- an exact isolation that costs no ANALYZE. Payloads are restored from a
temporary backup table via \texttt{UPDATE ... FROM} (the \texttt{pg\_mcv\_list}
type has no bytea cast, so the round-trip must happen inside the server).
Multiple capacity levels are measured with one object per (candidate, level)
via \texttt{ALTER STATISTICS ... SET STATISTICS}, again built by a single
ANALYZE at the maximum target. Protocol-M therefore costs one ANALYZE (for the
whole table) plus a cheap EXPLAIN per candidate, instead of two ANALYZEs per
candidate.

\subsection{Correctness of Masked Measurement}
\label{sec:measure-correct}

Masking must reproduce what a \emph{single} deployed statistic would deliver.
We validate this directly: for a statistic that the mask measurement predicts
at q-error $e$, we build \emph{only} that statistic on the real table and
re-measure. The singleton deployment reproduces the masked prediction (e.g.\
st.144: masked 1.04 vs.\ singleton 1.042; st.588 1.03 vs.\ 1.029; st.284 1.02
vs.\ 1.025, \S\ref{sec:e2e}). Together with the fact that a NULL payload is
ignored by the planner, this confirms Protocol-M is not an approximation but an
\emph{exact} isolation of each candidate's effect.

\subsection{Runtime: Measurement Is the Bottleneck, Not the Solve}
\label{sec:measure-runtime}

Table \ref{tab:measurement} shows the consequence. Using the measured linear
growth of ANALYZE time with the number of statistics on the table, Protocol-A
costs $2N$ ANALYZEs and stays proportional to $N$, while Protocol-M costs one
ANALYZE plus $N$ cheap EXPLAIN payloads. The gap widens with $N$: a $9\times$
saving at 10 candidates grows to $\sim$60$\times$ at 100 and $\sim$130$\times$
at 1000. For a single Census query the masked measurement completes in minutes
where the naive protocol would take hours. This is why the catalog-mask
protocol --- not the MILP --- is the enabling component that makes the massive
candidate $\times$ capacity space measurable, and why we emphasise that the
optimization (\S\ref{sec:rq4}) is the cheap part of the pipeline.

\subsection{RQ1: Does Capacity Materially Affect Repair Quality?}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Yes.} On the CENSUS \texttt{climate} table, the best single statistic
brings a high-error query to a q-error of roughly 2.1--2.3 at target 100,
1.2--2.1 at 1000, and $\approx$1.0 at 10000 (Table \ref{tab:capacity}). Target
100 cannot even represent the relevant 3-column correlation; target 1000
captures it but leaves residual error, and 10000 is near-exact. Capacity is
therefore not a storage knob that can be swept under the rug --- it sets the
estimation precision directly. This motivates treating
$(\text{columns},\text{capacity})$ as a joint decision (Prop.~\ref{prop:linear}).

\subsection{RQ2: Is One Statistic per Query Sufficient?}
\label{sec:rq2}

\textbf{Surprisingly, yes.} For each query we greedily rank non-overlapping
candidates by single q-error and measure the \emph{joint} q-error of keeping the
top-1/2/3. On stats\_CEB\_single the joint q-error at $k=2,3$ equals $k=1$
within 0.02--0.03; on the wide CENSUS table, \texttt{query.184} (4162 baseline,
165 candidates) and \texttt{query.382} (378, 56 candidates) are identical at
$k{=}1,2,3$ at both targets (Table \ref{tab:multi}). This is not an artefact of
a small candidate space: it holds on the widest, highest-error,
most-candidate-dense workload in our study.

This is not just anecdotal. Fig.~\ref{fig:sparsity} characterises the 
\emph{distribution} over the whole 632-query workload. For every query with
candidate statistics, we ask what fraction of the attainable single-statistic
improvement its best candidate captures (the ``top-1 coverage ratio''); the
ratio is meaningful for the 35 queries with real repair room (baseline $>1.2$),
and there it has \textbf{median 0.999}, with $94.3\%$ of such queries exceeding
$0.9$ and $77.1\%$ exceeding $0.99$. By baseline regime, a single candidate
reaches the estimation floor ($\le1.05$) for $94\%$ of near-baseline queries
(base 1--1.1) and $97\%$ of the genuinely repairable queries (base 2--10, $n{=}32$).
One dominant column cluster drives a single-table query's error, and one
well-chosen 3-column MCV captures it.

\subsection{RQ3: Does the MILP Beat a Budgeted Greedy?}
\label{sec:rq3}

We are careful here, and distinguish two greedy baselines. With a
\emph{generous} budget, the per-query-best greedy \emph{union} (ignoring the
budget) already equals the ILP solution (same 5 statistics, mean 1.021) --- on
a workload of independent single-table queries, greedy is already near-optimal
when nothing constrains it. The interesting question is under \emph{tight}
budgets, where a greedy that must stay within budget spends it on many cheap
statistics and cannot reallocate \emph{capacity}. Table \ref{tab:greedy} compares
the MILP against a budget-respecting greedy (each query's highest
improvement-per-byte statistic, within budget): the MILP wins at every budget,
and the gap is largest exactly when the budget is tightest (4.7 q-error points
at 5 KB, shrinking to 0.8 at 100 KB).

Qualitatively, the MILP's edge comes from capacity allocation: it spends the
budget upgrading already-chosen combinations to a higher target rather than
adding many under-powered ones (e.g.\ at 100 KB the MILP builds 17 statistics,
greedy builds 40, yet MILP is better). This is precisely the ``what and how
much'' resource-allocation view of \S\ref{sec:intro}: MILP is needed not because
greedy picks the wrong columns, but because the problem has a capacity-allocation
trade-off under tight budgets.

On the full 632-query workload (Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}), the budget is
visibly spent on capacity: the number of high-capacity selections grows with
budget (L10000: $1\to\cdots\to15$, L1000: $1\to\cdots\to15$), and the clearest
case, \texttt{posts(AnswerCount,ViewCount)}, upgrades from L100 to L1000 to
L10000 as the budget grows --- the same \emph{what}, more \emph{how much}. The
multi-metric view confirms that mean is outlier-dominated (median stays 1.000);
the real effect is \emph{tail repair}, with the maximum q-error falling from
4.7 to 1.1. On the huge-tail CENSUS top-10 the improvement is across the whole
distribution: baseline (mean / median / P90 / max) $=$
(1071.5 / 332.5 / 2542.9 / 4162.1) collapses to $(1.10 / 1.09 / 1.18 / 1.41)$.
We optimise mean but evaluate the full distribution; the headline is the tail
collapse, e.g.\ a 4162 $\to$ 1.4 maximum.

\subsection{RQ4: Does the Approach Scale?}
\label{sec:rq4}

\emph{The optimization is not the bottleneck.} On the full 468-query CENSUS
workload the worst-case (no-prune) MILP has 57{,}735 physical statistics, 92{,}568
options and 150{,}303 binary variables and is solved optimally by HiGHS in
$\approx$4.7s; with pruning it shrinks to 34{,}280 / 46{,}392 / 80{,}672 and
solves in $\approx$4.3s. Measurement (\S\ref{sec:measure}) dominates the
pipeline, not the solve.

\subsection{RQ5: Does Prediction Survive Real PostgreSQL Execution?}
\label{sec:rq5}

We split this into two parts (detailed in \S\ref{sec:e2e}).
\emph{(a) Are individual candidate measurements predictive?} Yes --- building a
single selected statistic alone reproduces its mask-measured q-error exactly.
\emph{(b) Are co-installed overlapping statistics independent?} No --- when
several overlapping MCVs coexist, the planner can switch a query to a
sub-optimal overlapping statistic. This second-order interaction is the one
place where the sparse model's prediction and the real system diverge, and it is
removed by the global-disjointness constraint (\S\ref{sec:p1}).

\subsection{RQ6: Does It Help Where Extended Statistics Cannot?}
\label{sec:rq6}

\textbf{No meaningful benefit --- as a sanity check.} On the join-heavy
\texttt{JOB} workload only 37/113 queries even have a multi-column selection
candidate and, after deduplication, just 7 distinct combinations exist; because
its errors are dominated by joins (which extended statistics cannot touch,
\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}), no amount of capacity tuning helps. This is the
expected null result for the negative control: the method does not optimise for
coverage's sake.

\section{End-to-End Validation}
\label{sec:e2e}

We close the loop by building ILP-selected sets on the real database and asking
a causal question with three stages: \emph{Is a single deployed statistic
predictable? Is a co-installed overlapping set predictable? And can we restore
predictability?} Each stage controls the previous one, so together they isolate
exactly where the model is sound and where it is not.

\subsection{Stage 1: A Single Deployed Statistic Is Predictable (RQ5a)}
\label{sec:e2e-singleton}

We first confirm that the mask-measured q-error of a single statistic matches
what it delivers when deployed alone. For the statistic
\texttt{posts(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} the catalog-mask measurement
predicts q-errors of 1.04 / 1.03 / 1.02 for queries st.144 / st.588 / st.284.
Building \emph{only} that statistic and re-measuring on the real table gives
1.042 / 1.029 / 1.025 --- matching the prediction almost exactly. This is the
grounding for \S\ref{sec:measure-correct}: per-candidate measurement is sound,
and it validates the economic premise of the whole pipeline (measure each
candidate in isolation, then compose).

\subsection{Stage 2: Co-installed Overlapping Statistics Break Predictability
(RQ5b)}
\label{sec:e2e-coinstalled}

Next we deploy the 2 MB ILP solution for stats\_CEB\_single: five statistics,
all pairwise-overlapping MCVs over \{AnswerCount, FavoriteCount, ViewCount,
PostTypeId\}, predicted mean q-error 1.021. Measured on the real table the mean
is \textbf{1.32} --- a 1.29$\times$ gap. Five of the eight queries match their
prediction (st.562: 1.003, st.308: 1.000, st.182: 1.000, st.398: 1.060, st.314:
1.008), but the three queries served by the \emph{same} statistic
\texttt{(AnswerCount,FavoriteCount,ViewCount)} degrade from their Stage-1
predictions (1.04/1.03/1.02) to 2.13 / 2.07 / 1.90.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2 for the very same statistic: alone it delivers
1.042; co-installed with four overlapping siblings it gives 2.13.
The \emph{statistic did not change, and its measurement was exact} (Stage 1);
what changed is that the planner now picks \emph{a different} overlapping
statistic for those queries. This is a second-order interaction among
co-installed overlapping statistics --- exactly the independence assumption that
the per-candidate sparse model relies on. It is not a failure to predict a
single statistic; it is a joint effect the per-candidate measurement cannot see
by construction.

\subsection{Stage 3: Global Disjointness Restores Exact Predictability}
\label{sec:p1}

We fix the gap with the Option-A model: a \emph{global-disjointness} constraint
($y_a+y_b\le 1$ whenever the column sets of $a,b$ overlap) forces the deployed
set to be pairwise column-disjoint, so the planner cannot cross-talk. Table
\ref{tab:p1} measures predicted vs E2E for both models at two budgets.

\subsection{The Causal Takeaway}
\label{sec:e2e-takeaway}

The three stages form a clean causal chain. (i) Per-candidate measurement is
sound, and a single deployed statistic is exactly predictable (Stage 1);
(ii) the only place prediction diverges is under co-installed \emph{overlapping}
statistics, where the planner's joint choice violates the per-candidate
independence assumption (Stage 2); and (iii) enforcing pairwise column
disjointness removes that interaction and restores ratio 1.000 (Stage 3), at a
quantified quality cost. This does not undermine the method --- it precisely
delimits where the sparse model holds, and provides a validated, deployable fix.
It is the empirical ``surprise'' that turns validation into a contribution; the
practical alternative when the quality cost of disjointness is unacceptable is
Option B: solve without the constraint, run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check,
drop any interfering statistic, and re-solve.

\section{Discussion and Open Questions}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Why Does Sparsity Occur? A Mechanistic Hypothesis}
\label{sec:disc-sparsity}

The sparse finding (\S\ref{sec:rq2}) is what makes the whole approach work, so
it deserves a mechanistic account -- though we stress it is a \emph{hypothesis},
not a theorem. Our view is that a single-table selection query's error is
typically dominated by \emph{one} correlated column cluster. Concretely: a
restriction clause is a conjunction of predicates, and its selectivity error is
largest where the columns are most strongly correlated (the independence
assumption fails hardest). The planner's default estimate for such a clause is
about as wrong as its worst correlated sub-clause; a single MCV over exactly
that dominant cluster removes the largest source of error, and residual errors
in the remaining (weakly-correlated) predicates contribute little to the total
q-error. Hence \emph{one} well-chosen statistic captures nearly all attainable
improvement, and a second non-overlapping statistic finds no independent
``second cluster'' worth capturing.

This hypothesis is consistent with every observation we report: it explains why
$k{=}2,3$ add nothing (RQ2), why the top-1 coverage CDF is sharply peaked near
1.0 (P4), why capacity \emph{upgrading} the dominant statistic matters more
than adding others (RQ3), and why the sparse cap costs no quality in the
ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}). It also predicts the boundary of our claim:
workloads where a query is genuinely driven by \emph{several} independent
correlated clusters would violate sparsity and warrant the multi-select model
($\S\ref{sec:formulation}$) instead. Characterising when that regime occurs is
an open question (below) and a natural direction for future work.

\subsection{What Is Being Optimised}
\label{sec:disc-objective}

We optimise a \emph{storage} budget (persistent bytes and estimation quality);
ANALYZE maintenance cost is handled separately because PostgreSQL couples its
sampling effort to the \emph{maximum} target on a relation. This keeps the
objective clean and lets us treat capacity as an allocation of persistent
storage rather than a proxy for maintenance cost. The budget-quality curves
(\S\ref{sec:rq3}) and the capacity-level mix (Fig.~\ref{fig:budgetquality}) are
the concrete evidence that a storage budget is, in practice, spent on the
``how much'' axis.

\subsection{The Interference Fix}
\label{sec:disc-fix}

The validated fix (Option A, global disjointness) is the trustworthy main model:
it restores exact predictability at a quantified quality cost (\S\ref{sec:p1}).
As a practical fallback (Option B), one can solve without the constraint and
then run a lightweight EXPLAIN-based check, drop any interfering statistic, and
re-solve. We recommend A as the default because it makes the deployment semantics
match the measurement model (\S\ref{sec:measure-correct}) --- the deployment is
then structurally independent, not just measured independently.

\subsection{Open Questions}

(1) \emph{Level monotonicity}: enforcing that a higher capacity never yields a
worse q-error could prune options and speed the solve, but is not required for
correctness. (2) \emph{Maintenance-aware selection}: jointly modelling ANALYZE
cost (as a function of the \emph{max} target) rather than storage alone is a
natural extension, replacing the storage-only budget with an end-to-end
maintenance budget. (3) \emph{When to upgrade vs.\ add}: our data show a smooth
capacity-only curve for stats\_CEB\_single and a large tail on CENSUS, but a
principled characterisation of the ``upgrade vs.\ add'' decision boundary is
open; a marginal-value heuristic (quality gained per storage unit for an upgrade
vs.\ a new statistic) would make the resource-allocation view of
\S\ref{sec:intro} more precise. (4) \emph{When does sparsity fail?}:
identifying workloads with multiple independent correlated clusters (the regime
where the multi-select model is needed) is the most important open question.
(5) \emph{Limits}: we consider MCV statistics on
single-table workloads; join workloads need other techniques, and the real
system has a limit on the number of statistics per relation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Extended-statistics selection should be treated as a joint \emph{which} and
\emph{how much} decision. Across single-table workloads we find the benefit is
strikingly sparse---one dominant statistic per query---which makes the
budget-constrained problem an exactly-linear MILP solvable in seconds at
full-workload scale, through a catalog-mask protocol that makes per-candidate,
per-capacity measurement feasible on wide tables. End-to-end validation shows
per-candidate prediction is sound and isolates the residual risk to planner
cross-talk among co-installed overlapping statistics; a global-disjointness
constraint restores exact predictability. Join-heavy workloads are the negative
control. The result is a principled, scalable, and honestly-validated recipe
for automating PostgreSQL extended-statistics deployment under a storage
budget.
